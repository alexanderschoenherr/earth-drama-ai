We Have to Grow Up Twice
The first unity is given. The second has to be made.

A little kid can meet another little kid and just play.

Not always, obviously. Children can be territorial, cruel, weird little dictators. But there is a kind of openness available before the adult world has fully explained itself.

You are you. I am me. Do you want to build this thing?

Great.

Then you grow up.

And growing up means learning the difference.

You learn what race means. Religion. Money. Status. Politics. Sex. Beauty. Danger. Betrayal. History. You learn which neighborhood means what. What people like us do. What people like them do.

You learn that some people lie. Some people will hurt you. Not everybody smiling at you likes you.

You learn enough to stop simply walking into the world.

This is not a tragedy.

The child does not possess some secret enlightenment because he does not know what a mortgage is.

Ignorance is not wisdom.

A huge part of becoming conscious is learning to distinguish: safe from dangerous, true from false, mine from yours, me from you.

Difference gives us power.

You need boundaries. You need an individual self before you can decide what that self believes. You need to be able to say no.

In fact, growing up almost seems to require a period of saying no to everything.

No, I don’t believe that.

No, I don’t want that life.

No, stop telling me who I am.

No, your music is terrible.

This last one appears to be essential.

Your parents can spend years playing perfectly acceptable music in the house and then, around adolescence, every note becomes an attack on your dignity.

Something real is happening underneath the melodrama.

You are trying to discover whether there is a self underneath the belonging.

At first:

I belong because this is where I am.

Then:

I need to know who I am apart from this.

And that separation matters.

A yes that cannot become no is not really a yes.

If you cannot disagree with your family, agreement means something different. If you cannot question the religion you inherited, belief means something different. If you cannot leave, staying means something different.

You have to learn no before another yes can become fully yours.

So maybe division is not exactly the problem.

Maybe the more accurate word is differentiation.

Differentiation is development.

Division is what happens when differentiation forgets relationship.

Relationships make this easier to see.

At first, another person can feel almost miraculously familiar.

You like the same things. You stay awake until four in the morning discovering additional things you also like.

The universe has clearly been planning this.

Then reality arrives.

This person is a separate human being.

They have needs you do not share. They interpret events incorrectly. They load the dishwasher in a way that raises serious questions about the future.

And suddenly what felt like unity contains difference.

One response is to make the difference disappear.

Become more like me. Want what I want. See what I see.

Another is to let difference become division.

You’re not like me, therefore you’re against me.

But there is another possibility.

I know you are not me.

I do not need you to be me.

The opposite of separation is not sameness.

It is relationship.

And this is where the whole thing starts getting strange.

Because the beginning and the end can sometimes look almost identical.

A child trusts.

A mature adult trusts.

From across the room, same behavior.

Not remotely the same achievement.

The child may trust because betrayal has not yet become imaginable. The adult may trust while knowing perfectly well what betrayal is.

That is not a return to the same place.

Trust after betrayal is not the same trust.

Belonging after individuation is not the same belonging.

Wonder after explanation is not the same wonder.

Maybe we are not trying to recover innocence.

Maybe we are trying to recover what innocence made possible.

To rebuild what we lost in forms that no longer depend on what we didn’t know.

Trust that no longer depends on ignorance.

Love that no longer depends on idealization.

Belonging that no longer depends on conformity.

Hope that no longer depends on believing nothing bad will happen.

That is harder.

It can also be faked.

“I don’t believe in labels.”

Maybe that means you can see the person beyond the category.

Maybe it means you don’t want to learn what the category is telling you.

“We’re all one.”

Maybe.

Sometimes unity is avoidance wearing linen.

“I’ve transcended my ego.”

Okay.

Can anyone criticize you?

The ego may have simply changed outfits.

Whatever comes after differentiation has to contain what differentiation taught us. Otherwise it isn’t integration. It’s escape.

The connection has to survive reality.

And reality gets complicated.

Someone you love hurts you.

They loved me.

They failed me.

Some of what they did came from their own wounds.

That explains something and excuses nothing.

I may forgive them.

I may still need distance.

I may miss them and still be furious.

That is a lot to hold.

The mind would often prefer something cheaper.

They’re evil.

Or:

It wasn’t that bad.

One removes the love. The other removes the harm.

Either way, reality becomes easier.

We do this constantly.

I am the good one. They are the bad one.

My family was wonderful. My family ruined me.

Pick the truth that keeps the structure standing. Delete whatever complicates it.

But maybe maturity is not learning how to make reality simpler.

Maybe it is increasing your capacity for reality.

Reality gets bigger.

Can you get bigger with it?

Can you let the whole thing be true?

That does not mean becoming endlessly vague. There is a version of “everything is complicated” that is just cowardice with excellent vocabulary.

Sometimes someone lied.

Sometimes a boundary was crossed.

Sometimes the answer is no.

Sometimes you leave.

Integration is not refusing to judge.

It is refusing to falsify reality in order to judge.

I understand why you did this. It was still wrong.

I know you love me. I still need to leave.

I see your humanity. This is still unacceptable.

You do not have to make someone a monster in order to protect yourself from them. You do not have to erase what happened in order to recognize their humanity.

And maybe one of the stranger forms of freedom is this:

I no longer need to be against you in order to know that I am not you.

That brought me back to the pattern I started with:

Unconscious unity.

Conscious division.

Conscious unity.

Except by then I didn’t quite mean the same thing anymore.

Maybe it was closer to:

Unity.

Differentiation.

Integration.

Or belonging, separation, chosen belonging.

Or a given yes, a necessary no, a chosen yes.

Different ways of seeing the same movement.

The first unity exists partly because the differences have not fully appeared yet.

The second has to contain them.

You have to become somebody before you can belong without disappearing.

You have to become capable of leaving before staying can become a choice.

You have to know where the boundary is before crossing it can become intimacy instead of invasion.

You have to become separate enough for relationship to become real.

And once I saw that, I started noticing the same shape elsewhere.

Not the same process. Childhood, romance, psychology, knowledge, and civilization are not secretly one thing.

But they may keep encountering a similar problem.

Differentiation creates capability.

And every new distinction creates a new relationship that has to be managed.

Once there is me and you, we need relationship.

Once knowledge breaks into specialties, the specialties have to speak to one another.

The parts become more capable by becoming more distinct.

Then the whole inherits a new problem:

Can the parts still belong to one another?

You can become more sophisticated and less whole at the same time.

The problem is not that the parts became different.

The problem is when difference becomes disconnection.

Wholeness does not ask the parts to become less themselves.

It asks them to become capable of relationship.

Maybe that is why some of the highest human capacities look strangely like things we had before we knew enough to lose them.

Someone who knows nothing can say, “I don’t know.”

Someone who has spent twenty years becoming an expert can say exactly the same thing.

Same sentence.

Entirely different journey.

Someone can be gentle because they do not know how to fight. Someone else can be gentle because they know perfectly well how to fight and do not need to.

Someone can forgive because they cannot confront anyone. Someone else can forgive after confronting the whole truth.

Someone can belong because they never learned how to leave.

Someone else can leave, build a self, and one day return.

Maybe even hear their parents’ terrible music again and realize, irritatingly, that some of it was actually pretty good.

The return matters because return is no longer compulsory.

The second yes means something because no became possible.

Maybe that is what I mean by conscious unity.

Not everybody becoming the same.

Not forgetting what you learned.

Not pretending difference disappeared.

A wholeness capable of difference.

A relationship that does not require disappearance.

A self that does not require opposition.

The first unity is given.

The second has to be made.
