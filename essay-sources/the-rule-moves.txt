The Rule Moves

Donald Trump and what happens when loyalty becomes stronger than principle

Donald Trump believes in law and order.

He has been telling us for years.

In January 2025, Trump granted sweeping clemency to the January 6 defendants, including people convicted of violently assaulting police officers at the Capitol. A few months later, during protests against his immigration policies in Los Angeles, he warned demonstrators that if they spat at law enforcement, “WE WILL HIT.”

Donald Trump also believes very strongly in free speech.

On his first day back in office, he signed an executive order called Restoring Freedom of Speech and Ending Federal Censorship. In July 2026, after ABC and NBC declined to carry one of his political speeches, Trump said their broadcast licenses should be revoked.

He believes the federal government should never be weaponized against political opponents.

That was another Day One executive order: Ending the Weaponization of the Federal Government.

Reuters later documented at least 470 people, organizations, and institutions targeted in Trump’s campaign of retribution, including prosecutors, law firms, media organizations, civil servants, universities, and others he regarded as enemies.

He believes in merit.

His administration formally declared that federal hiring should focus on merit, practical skill, and constitutional service. At the same time, job candidates were subjected to intense political vetting, including questions about their support for Trump and, in at least one reported interview, the moment of their “MAGA revelation.”

And we have barely started.

There is executive power.

Before becoming president, Trump watched Barack Obama use executive orders and called them “major power grabs.” He called governing by executive order a “basic disaster.”

Then he became president.

By his first term, he was issuing executive orders faster than Obama had over comparable stretches of his presidency.

There is draining the swamp.

Trump entered national politics promising to break the grip of Washington influence-peddlers. When he returned to office in 2025, one of his early acts was to eliminate Biden-era ethics restrictions that limited major gifts from lobbyists and restricted the revolving door between lobbying and government.

And then there are the family businesses.

Since Trump returned to office, Reuters estimates that the Trump family has made roughly $2.3 billion from crypto ventures.

One of them, World Liberty Financial, is backed by Trump and his family. In August 2026, a federal regulator inside Trump’s own administration conditionally approved it for a national trust-bank charter.

Drain the swamp.

Open a bank.

There are elites.

Trump has spent years railing against them.

His current administration contains 57 officials worth at least $100 million each—more than four times as many as the previous three presidential administrations combined.

Which tells us something useful.

If elite simply means rich and powerful, this makes very little sense.

So apparently elite means something else.

The wrong rich and powerful people.

There is the deep state.

For years, the phrase described unelected government officials supposedly imposing their own politics instead of carrying out the will of the elected president.

Then Trump’s White House began screening job applicants for allegiance to MAGA.

It asked how they had supported Trump.

It examined political histories.

It asked at least one applicant when the person had experienced a “MAGA revelation.”

And his administration created a new category of policy-influencing federal employees designed to make it easier to remove people who would not carry out the president’s agenda.

Their ideological bureaucracy is a deep state.

Ours is accountability.

There is democracy.

Trump still insists that the 2020 election he lost was stolen.

In 2025, he issued federal pardons to Rudy Giuliani, Sidney Powell, and dozens of other allies accused of participating in efforts to overturn that loss.

People connected to those efforts have since returned to positions of influence over election policy.

And Trump has made “election integrity” a central issue again.

There is even mail voting.

Trump has called mail-in ballots “cheating.”

He has attacked mail voting for years.

He has pushed government action to restrict it.

And in March 2026, Donald Trump voted by mail.

There is identity politics.

There is victimhood.

There is political correctness.

There is cancel culture.

There is truth and fake news.

There is character.

There is war.

There is golf.

There is, somehow, always golf.

And then there is the gold.

Trump returned to the White House and began covering the Oval Office in gold accents while pursuing an enormous ballroom project on the site of the demolished East Wing.

He originally insisted the ballroom would cost taxpayers nothing.

An internal contractor estimate later put the project at roughly $600 million, with about half expected to come from tax dollars. His broader White House construction program is now expected to cost more than $900 million, with taxpayers covering most of it.

So yes.

There is the literal money.

But the image is almost better.

An administration preaching waste, restraint, efficiency, and cutting excess, operating inside a presidency increasingly decorated in the visual language of abundance, monumentality, and gold.

At some point, the word hypocrisy begins to feel almost insufficient.

Fine.

Trump is a hypocrite.

This is not exactly breaking news.

His critics have been pointing out contradictions like these for a decade. Every few weeks, another one gets passed around as though this might finally be the contradiction so geometrically perfect that the whole thing will collapse.

Look what he said then.

Look what he is doing now.

Look.

Look.

And millions of people look.

And then they vote for him anyway.

Which suggests a more interesting question.

Why doesn’t this work?

The theory behind exposing hypocrisy is simple.

He says he believes X.

Then he does the opposite.

You put the two clips next to each other.

Something is supposed to happen.

Credibility collapses.

Support disappears.

The spell breaks.

Except the last part keeps not happening.

And the problem is not simply that people haven’t noticed.

Plenty of Trump voters know he behaves in ways they would never accept from someone they hated. Some dislike parts of his behavior and vote for him anyway because they want his judges, immigration policies, abortion policies, taxes, cultural posture, or simply want Democrats to lose.

Fine.

That explains choosing a politician despite his flaws.

It does not explain something stranger:

why the standard itself keeps moving.

Watch the language.

When political opponents use government power against Trump, it becomes weaponization.

When Trump uses government power against people he regards as enemies, it becomes justice.

When government pressure touches conservative speech, it becomes censorship.

When government pressure is directed at hostile media or institutions, it becomes accountability.

When progressives organize politically around race, gender, or sexuality, it becomes identity politics.

When MAGA organizes around patriotism, Christianity, traditional gender roles, nationalism, cultural grievance, red hats, flags, shared enemies, and a powerful sense of who the real Americans are, that is simply America.

Their identity is an identity.

Ours is reality.

When people on the left describe themselves as mistreated, conservatives often call it a culture of victimhood.

When Trump describes himself and his movement as persecuted by prosecutors, bureaucrats, judges, media organizations, universities, election officials, and political enemies, it becomes persecution.

Their grievance is weakness.

Ours is evidence that the fight is righteous.

Their rich and powerful people are elites.

Our rich and powerful people are successful.

Their billionaire is an oligarch.

Ours is a businessman.

Their ideological bureaucracy is the deep state.

Our ideological bureaucracy is a government finally answering to the president.

Their political hiring is politicization.

Our loyalty screening is putting the right people in the room.

Their executive action is overreach.

Ours is a president finally using the power voters gave him.

Their protest is disorder.

Our disorder has a story.

Their attempt to overturn an election threatens democracy.

Our attempt to overturn an election becomes a fight for election integrity.

Their doubts about an election undermine the system.

Our doubts prove the system cannot be trusted.

Their preferred voting method is suspicious.

Our ballot arrives through the mail.

Their investigations are justice.

Investigations of us are persecution.

Their falsehood is propaganda.

Our falsehood becomes exaggeration, trolling, a joke, something taken out of context, something the media deliberately misunderstood—or simply not the thing we should be talking about right now.

And yet the movement remains intensely preoccupied with truth.

Fake news.

Hoaxes.

Propaganda.

The lies they tell you.

What they don’t want you to know.

That matters.

Because the deeper problem may not be that truth has been abandoned.

It may be that truth itself has become tribal in practice while remaining sacred in rhetoric.

The question quietly changes from:

Is this true?

to:

Who is telling me this?

And then the answer to the second question begins doing some of the work that used to belong to the first.

The details differ.

The sorting does not.

First identify the team.

Then determine the morality.

And something peculiar keeps happening to the name.

Sometimes the violation does not get excused.

It gets renamed.

This is not censorship.

This is accountability.

This is not retaliation.

This is justice.

This is not identity politics.

This is patriotism.

This is not victimhood.

This is persecution.

This is not an elite.

This is a successful person.

This is not politicizing government.

This is defeating the deep state.

This is not executive overreach.

This is strength.

This is not election denial.

This is election integrity.

And now a person does not even have to experience himself as abandoning the principle.

He still believes in free speech.

He still believes in law and order.

He still believes in merit.

He still hates identity politics.

He still despises elites.

He still opposes government weaponization.

He still believes in democracy.

He still believes in truth.

The rule has moved just enough to keep the allegiance exactly where it was.

The rule moves. The allegiance remains.

This is where American politics begins to look less like political philosophy and more like sports.

Our player is aggressive.

Their player is dirty.

Our fans are passionate.

Their fans are animals.

Our coach is fighting for every inch.

Their coach is cheating.

Same field.

Different jersey.

Suddenly a different theory of justice.

Republicans and Democrats still believe different things. Policies matter. Ideology matters. Elections have consequences.

But political parties are also identities.

They become:

my people

and

those people.

And once politics becomes identity, the reasoning can quietly reverse.

We imagine:

I believe this, so I support this person.

But sometimes it becomes:

I support this person, so what he just did must somehow fit with what I believe.

Trump did not invent this.

Humanity had achieved partisan hypocrisy long before the escalator.

Democrats do it.

Republicans do it.

Liberals do it.

Conservatives do it.

Every human group eventually discovers that its own mistakes arrive with context while the other side’s mistakes arrive with character.

But Trump has become something more than an ordinary beneficiary of that tendency.

Ordinary partisanship bends the rule for the team.

Trump can sometimes bend the team—and therefore the rule—around the leader.

Political scientists have actually tested this. In experiments, strong Trump supporters moved toward positions after being told Trump supported them—even when those positions cut against conventional conservative ideology. Other research has found that politicians who opposed Trump could come to be perceived by Republican activists as less conservative despite highly conservative voting records.

Think about that.

Not merely:

I disagree with this conservative.

But:

Perhaps he is no longer really conservative.

The word itself begins to move.

Trump becomes something like a movable center of ideological gravity.

The arrow can run backward.

principle → leader

can become

leader → principle.

And that is different from ordinary hypocrisy.

A hypocrite breaks the rule.

A sufficiently powerful leader can sometimes help change what the group believes the rule was.

You can see the same movement in moral judgment.

In 2011, only about 30% of white evangelical Protestants said a politician who behaved immorally in private could still behave ethically in public office.

By 2016, as Trump became the Republican nominee, that number had risen to 72%.

The moral principle did not disappear.

Its relationship to the political choice changed.

The argument over “America First” shows the same pressure in foreign policy.

Trump spent years promising to keep America out of unnecessary foreign wars.

Then he took the United States into a war with Iran.

The decision split parts of MAGA. Some prominent America First voices denounced it as a betrayal.

But the Republican Party largely rallied around the operation at the start. In the first Reuters/Ipsos polling after the strikes, Republican voters approved of them by 55% to 13%. As the war dragged on, casualties mounted, gas prices rose, and the conflict became increasingly unpopular even inside the party.

The revealing thing is not that no Republicans resisted him.

Many did.

The revealing thing is the argument itself:

What does America First mean now that Trump has done this?

Who decides?

The principle?

The movement?

The circumstances?

Or Donald Trump?

Every contradiction becomes a small experiment.

Not merely:

Is Trump a hypocrite?

But:

Which one moves first?

The loyalty?

Or the rule?

Trump’s hypocrisies begin to look less like random failures of consistency and more like measurements of allegiance.

Every contradiction asks the same question.

How much can the principle move before the loyalty does?

And if loyalty keeps winning, we should probably ask what loyalty is giving people.

Because consistency is not competing against nothing.

Politics gives people belonging.

These are my people.

They see what I see.

They are angry about the same things.

They know the jokes.

They know the enemies.

They wear the same hat.

That can sound trivial until you remember that human beings have spent most of history arranging enormous portions of their lives around the need to belong somewhere.

Politics can also give people dignity.

This may be one of Trump’s greatest emotional gifts to his supporters.

Imagine living for years with the feeling that respectable culture has placed you in the dumb section of the country.

Your religion is embarrassing.

Your town is irrelevant.

Your patriotism is corny.

Your masculinity is toxic.

Your opinions prove you are uneducated.

You are the people who need to be corrected by people with better degrees, better vocabulary, better neighborhoods, and better taste.

Some of those grievances are real.

Some are exaggerated.

Some have been deliberately cultivated.

It almost doesn’t matter.

Humiliation hurts before it becomes a political theory.

Then a man appears and says, essentially:

No.

They are the disgrace.

They lied to you.

They looked down on you.

You were right to be angry.

And I am going to make them listen.

That is not a tax deduction.

That is restoration.

And then there is certainty.

Reality, unfortunately, is a mess.

Institutions can be corrupt in one place and trustworthy in another.

Your side can behave badly.

Your enemy can occasionally be right.

A war can contain genuine danger, political incentives, moral horror, strategic necessity, stupidity, and uncertainty all at once.

A person can be wrong about something important and right about something else five minutes later.

The people you admire can disappoint you.

The people you despise can occasionally behave honorably.

This is an extremely inconvenient arrangement.

Human beings have repeatedly proposed alternatives.

Good.

Evil.

Patriot.

Traitor.

Truth.

Lie.

People.

Elite.

Strength.

Weakness.

Decline.

Restoration.

Now the world is legible again.

You know where you stand.

You know who you are.

And perhaps most importantly, you know that you are one of the good people.

That is where the trap closes.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to be good.

We should probably keep that one.

The danger begins when goodness stops functioning as a standard and becomes an identity.

If I say:

We are the good people

and then my side does something wrong, I have a problem.

I can say:

We did something wrong.

Or I can protect the identity:

What we did must not really have been wrong.

Maybe it was necessary.

Maybe they started it.

Maybe the media distorted it.

Maybe they would have done worse.

Maybe the circumstances were different.

Maybe it has another name when we do it.

Because if we are good is something that can never be disproved by anything we actually do, then goodness is no longer judging the group.

The group is deciding what counts as good.

That is an enormous reversal.

And suddenly all that surviving hypocrisy makes a lot more sense.

A contradiction is only politically expensive when consistency is more valuable than what the contradiction protects.

But consistency may be competing with belonging.

With dignity.

With certainty.

With my friends.

With my family.

With my church.

With my town.

With my idea of masculinity.

With my understanding of America.

With ten years of political arguments.

With every person I told was an idiot.

With my belief that I am perceptive.

With my belief that I am independent.

With my belief that I am good.

That is a hell of a competition.

And none of this means Democrats and Republicans are doing the same thing.

They aren’t.

A human weakness can be universal without its political expression being equal.

The susceptibility is human.

The scale, structure, leadership, consequences, and present political reality still have to be judged honestly.

That distinction matters.

Otherwise “everybody does it” becomes one more way of refusing to look at what somebody is actually doing.

The point is not that everyone is equally guilty.

The point is that the machinery itself is not Republican.

It is human.

That is not an acquittal.

It is a warning.

Because one of the easiest ways to become morally blind is to become certain that moral blindness is something that happens to those people.

It is easy to recognize tribal morality when the tribe is wearing a red hat.

It becomes harder when the tribe has your politics.

Your language.

Your moral priorities.

Your trusted sources.

Your heroes.

Your enemies.

Your symbols.

Your history.

Your very good reasons.

I am sure mine come with excellent context.

We began with an easy question:

How can they not see the hypocrisy?

The more useful question may be:

Where do I stop seeing mine?

Believing in free speech when your people are speaking is easy.

Believing in accountability when your enemy is being investigated is easy.

Believing in truth when the truth confirms what you already believe is easy.

Believing in law and order when the person breaking the law disgusts you is easy.

Believing in democracy when the election produces the result you wanted is easy.

Believing in merit when the person who benefits looks like the kind of person you imagined merit producing is easy.

Believing that power must have limits when your enemy holds the power is easy.

That is not where the principle is tested.

The test comes when the person is yours.

When the speaker disgusts you.

When the investigation reaches someone you love.

When the fact makes your side look bad.

When the election disappoints you.

When the rule protects somebody you cannot stand.

When applying the rule costs your side power.

When admitting what happened costs you pride.

That is when we find out whether we actually believed in the rule.

A principle becomes real when it can judge your own side.
