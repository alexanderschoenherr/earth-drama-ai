The Rule Moves

Donald Trump and what hypocrisy reveals about political loyalty

Donald Trump believes in law and order.

He has been telling us for years.

In January 2025, Trump granted sweeping clemency to the January 6 defendants, including people convicted of violently assaulting police officers at the Capitol. A few months later, during protests against his immigration policies in Los Angeles, he warned demonstrators that if they spat at law enforcement, “WE WILL HIT.”

Donald Trump also believes very strongly in free speech.

On his first day back in office, he signed an executive order called Restoring Freedom of Speech and Ending Federal Censorship. In July 2026, after ABC and NBC declined to carry one of his political speeches on their primary broadcast channels, Trump said their station licenses should be revoked.

He believes the federal government should never be weaponized against political opponents.

That was another Day One executive order: Ending the Weaponization of the Federal Government. Reuters later documented hundreds of people, organizations, and institutions targeted for retaliation during his second term, including prosecutors, law firms, media organizations, civil servants, and other perceived enemies.

Not every investigation of a political opponent is illegitimate simply because the target is a political opponent.

Of course not.

But the contrast between the principle and the pattern is difficult to miss.

He believes in merit.

His administration formally declared that federal hiring should focus on merit, practical skill, and constitutional service. At the same time, job candidates were subjected to unusually intense political vetting, including questions about their support for Trump and, in at least one reported interview, the moment of their “MAGA revelation.”

And we have barely started.

There is executive power.

There is “draining the swamp.”

There are elites.

There is victimhood.

There is identity politics.

There is truth and fake news.

There is fiscal restraint.

There is personal responsibility.

There is character.

There is war.

There is golf.

There is, somehow, always golf.

And then there is the gold.

Trump returned to the White House and began covering the Oval Office in gold accents. He has also pursued an enormous ballroom project whose reported cost has climbed into the hundreds of millions of dollars. Much of the ballroom has been described as privately funded, so the cheap version of the argument—he complains about government waste and spends taxpayer money on gold—is not necessarily the accurate one.

The image is more interesting anyway.

An administration obsessed with waste, restraint, efficiency, and cutting excess, operating inside a presidency increasingly decorated in the visual language of abundance, monumentality, and gold.

At some point, the word hypocrisy begins to feel almost insufficient.

Fine.

Trump is a hypocrite.

This is not exactly breaking news.

His critics have been pointing out contradictions like these for a decade. Every few weeks, another one gets passed around as though this might finally be the contradiction so geometrically perfect that the whole thing will collapse.

Look what he said then.

Look what he is doing now.

Look.

Look.

And millions of people look.

And then they vote for him anyway.

Which suggests a more interesting question.

Why doesn’t this work?

The theory behind exposing hypocrisy is simple.

He says he believes X.

Then he does the opposite.

You put the two clips next to each other.

Something is supposed to happen.

Credibility collapses. Support disappears. The spell breaks.

Except the last part keeps not happening.

And perhaps the problem is not simply that people haven’t noticed.

Some Trump supporters plainly do notice. Some dislike his behavior. Some think he is personally immoral. Some probably think he is a gigantic pain in the ass.

They vote for him anyway because they prefer his immigration policy, judges, taxes, abortion policy, foreign policy, cultural posture, or simply because they believe the alternative is worse.

That is not necessarily hypocrisy.

Politics is made of tradeoffs. We all support people who do things we dislike because we care more about something else.

The stranger thing begins when the standard itself appears to move.

Watch the language.

When political opponents use government power against Trump, it becomes weaponization.

When Trump uses government power against people he regards as enemies, it can become justice.

When progressives organize politically around race, gender, or sexuality, it becomes identity politics.

When MAGA organizes around patriotism, Christianity, traditional gender roles, nationalism, cultural grievance, red hats, flags, shared enemies, and a powerful sense of who the real Americans are, that is simply America.

When people on the left describe themselves as mistreated, conservatives often call it a culture of victimhood.

When Trump describes himself and his movement as persecuted, the category becomes persecution.

Their rich and powerful people are elites.

Our rich and powerful people are successful.

The point is not that every pair is identical.

They aren’t.

Context matters. Scale matters. Law matters.

But something peculiar keeps happening to the name.

Sometimes the violation does not get excused.

It gets renamed.

This is not censorship.

This is accountability.

This is not retaliation.

This is justice.

This is not identity politics.

This is patriotism.

And now a person does not even have to experience himself as abandoning the principle.

He still believes in free speech.

He still believes in law and order.

He still believes in merit.

The rule has moved just enough to keep the allegiance exactly where it was.

The rule moves. The allegiance remains.

This is where American politics begins to look less like political philosophy and more like sports.

Our player is aggressive.

Their player is dirty.

Our fans are passionate.

Their fans are animals.

Same field. Different jersey. Suddenly a different theory of justice.

Republicans and Democrats still believe different things. Policies matter. Ideology matters. Elections have consequences.

But political parties are also identities.

They can become:

my people

and

those people.

And once politics becomes identity, the reasoning can quietly reverse.

We imagine:

I believe this, so I support this person.

But sometimes it becomes:

I support this person, so what he just did must somehow fit with what I believe.

Trump did not invent this.

Humanity had achieved partisan hypocrisy long before the escalator.

What Trump appears unusually capable of doing is becoming part of the machinery by which the group decides what the principle means.

Political scientists have actually tested versions of this. In experiments, some strong Trump supporters moved toward a position after being told Trump supported it, even when the position cut against conventional conservative ideology.

That does not mean Trump controls what every Republican thinks. He doesn’t. People have real beliefs, and plenty of those beliefs resist leaders.

It means the arrow can sometimes run backward.

principle → leader

can become

leader → principle.

The argument over “America First” makes this unusually visible.

For years, one of Trump’s signature promises was that America would stop entering unnecessary foreign wars. The United States is now in a war with Iran. Some prominent America First voices have accused Trump of betraying the movement, while many Republican voters continue to understand his handling of the war as consistent with the same America First project.

The revealing question is not simply whether the intervention is justified.

It is:

Who gets to decide what America First means?

The principle?

The movement?

The circumstances?

Or Donald Trump?

Every contradiction becomes a small experiment.

Which one moves first?

The loyalty?

Or the rule?

And if loyalty keeps winning, we should probably ask what loyalty is giving people.

Because consistency is not competing against nothing.

Politics gives people belonging.

These are my people.

They see what I see.

They are angry about the same things.

They know the jokes.

They know the enemies.

They wear the same hat.

That can sound trivial until you remember that human beings have spent most of history arranging enormous portions of their lives around the need to belong somewhere.

Politics can also give people dignity.

This may be one of Trump’s greatest emotional gifts to his supporters.

Imagine living for years with the feeling—accurate or not—that respectable culture has placed you in the dumb section of the country.

Your religion is embarrassing.

Your town is irrelevant.

Your patriotism is corny.

Your masculinity is toxic.

Your opinions prove you are uneducated.

You are the people who need to be corrected by people with better degrees, better vocabulary, better neighborhoods, and better taste.

Whether that perception is entirely accurate is almost beside the point.

Humiliation does not need a perfect theory of its origin to hurt.

Then a man appears and says, essentially:

No.

They are the disgrace.

They lied to you.

They looked down on you.

You were right to be angry.

And I am going to make them listen.

That is not a tax deduction.

That is restoration.

And then there is certainty.

Reality, unfortunately, is a mess.

Institutions can be corrupt in one place and trustworthy in another. Your side can behave badly. Your enemy can occasionally be right. A war can contain genuine danger, political incentives, moral horror, strategic necessity, stupidity, and uncertainty all at once.

This is an extremely inconvenient arrangement.

Human beings have repeatedly proposed alternatives.

Good.

Evil.

Patriot.

Traitor.

Truth.

Lie.

People.

Elite.

Decline.

Restoration.

Now the world is legible again.

You know where you stand.

You know who you are.

And perhaps most importantly, you know that you are one of the good people.

That is where the trap closes.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to be good.

We should probably keep that one.

The danger begins when goodness stops functioning as a standard and becomes an identity.

If I say:

We are the good people

and then my side does something wrong, I have a problem.

I can say:

We did something wrong.

Or I can protect the identity:

What we did must not really have been wrong.

Maybe it was necessary.

Maybe they started it.

Maybe the media distorted it.

Maybe the circumstances were different.

Maybe it has another name when we do it.

And now all that surviving hypocrisy begins to make more sense.

A contradiction is only politically expensive when consistency is more valuable than what the contradiction protects.

But consistency may be competing with belonging.

With dignity.

With certainty.

With my friends.

With my family.

With my understanding of America.

With ten years of political arguments.

With my belief that I am perceptive.

With my belief that I am good.

That is a hell of a competition.

None of this requires us to pretend Democrats and Republicans behave identically.

They don’t.

Political movements can share a human vulnerability and express it in different degrees, in different forms, with different consequences.

The point is not that everyone is equally guilty.

The point is that the machinery is not Republican.

It is human.

That is not an acquittal.

It is a warning.

It is easy to recognize tribal morality when the tribe is wearing a red hat.

It becomes harder when the tribe has your politics.

Your language.

Your moral priorities.

Your trusted sources.

Your heroes.

Your enemies.

Your very good reasons.

I am sure mine come with excellent context.

We began with an easy question:

How can they not see the hypocrisy?

The more useful question may be:

Where do I stop seeing mine?

Believing in free speech when your people are speaking is easy.

Believing in accountability when your enemy is being investigated is easy.

Believing in truth when the truth confirms what you already believe is easy.

Believing in law and order when the person breaking the law disgusts you is easy.

The test comes when the person is yours.

When the fact makes your side look bad.

When applying the rule costs your side power.

When admitting what happened costs you pride.

That is when we find out whether we actually believed in the rule.

A principle becomes real when it can judge your own side.
