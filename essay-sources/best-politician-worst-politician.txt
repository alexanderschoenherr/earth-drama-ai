The Best Politician Is the Worst Politician

How did Donald Trump become the perfect politician—and what does that reveal about a civilization getting better at the wrong things?

Donald Trump may be the best politician in America.

That is not a compliment.

He has won the presidency twice, reshaped a major political party, and spent more than a decade making American politics orbit around him.

There are two meanings buried inside the word politician.

One is simply a person entrusted with political power: a president, senator, governor, mayor. Someone elected to represent people, govern institutions, solve collective problems, and use power in service of the public.

Then there is the other meaning.

You know it immediately when someone says, He’s such a politician.

A dealmaker. An operator. Someone who knows what to say, what not to say, who to flatter, who to attack, which position to take, which principle to abandon, which alliance to make, which truth to stretch, and which crowd to please in order to get what he wants.

By that definition, Trump is not a failure of politics.

He is a virtuoso.

And that is precisely what can make him such a failure by the first definition.

The best politician is the worst politician.

Because the skills required to master politics are not necessarily the skills required to fulfill the purpose of politics.

And that problem is much bigger than Donald Trump.

⸻

Trump built much of his political identity around the idea that he was not a politician at all.

He was the outsider.

The businessman.

The guy who couldn’t be bought.

The guy who didn’t sound like Washington because he didn’t belong to Washington.

He said what other people wouldn’t say. He insulted people politicians weren’t supposed to insult. He violated rules politicians were supposed to respect. He contradicted himself, exaggerated, boasted, improvised, and broke through the careful language that had come to characterize American public life.

For many of his supporters, this felt like honesty.

Not necessarily factual honesty.

Something closer to personal honesty.

That’s just Trump.

And those are two very different things.

Authenticity asks:

Is this really you?

Truth asks:

Is what you’re saying real?

A person can be authentically himself without authentically describing reality.

You can sincerely be a bullshitter.

You can genuinely be manipulative.

You can authentically exaggerate.

And once those qualities become part of an established personality, evidence against the person can become evidence for the persona.

He exaggerates?

That’s Trump.

He boasts?

That’s Trump.

He attacks?

That’s Trump.

He says the thing nobody else would say?

Exactly.

The violation confirms the character.

And that creates an extraordinary political advantage.

The traditional politician is punished when the mask slips.

Trump made the slipping mask part of the mask.

⸻

There is another advantage to being this kind of politician.

You can hide something by concealing it.

But sometimes you can also hide something by putting it everywhere.

Traditional political scandal has a familiar structure.

There is the public face.

There is the secret behavior.

Someone discovers the secret.

The curtain is pulled back.

Exposure changes the story.

But what happens when the politician says the quiet part out loud?

What happens when the appetite for power, loyalty, revenge, money, attention, or advantage is visible from the beginning?

Something strange happens.

The revelation has already happened.

You cannot shock people by revealing what they have already watched for years.

Brazenness becomes a kind of camouflage.

Not because the behavior stops mattering.

Because exposure loses some of its force.

There is no curtain to pull back.

The room was built with glass walls.

And for some supporters, the openness can even reverse the moral meaning of the behavior.

At least he admits it.

At least you know what you’re getting.

All politicians do this. He’s just honest about it.

Now the comparison is no longer between good behavior and bad behavior.

It is between hidden bad behavior and visible bad behavior.

And visible bad behavior can begin to feel like integrity.

This may be one of Trump’s great political gifts: shamelessness in a system that often relies on shame.

If the punishment for violating a norm is embarrassment, there is power in refusing to be embarrassed.

If the punishment for scandal is exposure, there is power in already being exposed.

If the punishment for seeming self-interested is being called a politician, there is power in convincing millions of people that your shamelessness proves you aren’t one.

Trump does not merely survive scandal.

He metabolizes it.

The archetypal politician conquered American politics by weaponizing America’s contempt for politicians.

But Trump is not really the subject of this essay.

He is the clue.

⸻

Politics is supposed to be a means.

We organize power because millions of human beings have to live together.

We need laws, schools, courts, roads, rights, borders, clean water, public safety, and some way of settling conflicts without killing one another.

Underneath all that machinery is an almost embarrassingly simple purpose:

help people thrive.

Politics cannot make a human being wise or loving or fulfilled. It cannot manufacture meaning.

But it can help create the conditions in which people have some real chance to live safe, free, meaningful, flourishing lives.

That is the end.

Everything else is machinery.

Parties.

Elections.

Campaigns.

Fundraising.

Polling.

Messaging.

Debates.

Coalitions.

Media appearances.

Political strategy.

All of these things exist because human beings need some way to decide who gets power and what they should do with it.

But mechanisms develop lives of their own.

They become professions.

Professions develop expertise.

Expertise develops measurement.

Measurement creates incentives.

Incentives create optimization.

And eventually it becomes possible to become extraordinarily good at the machinery while becoming increasingly detached from the reason the machinery was built.

You can master the election without mastering governance.

You can master political communication without mastering truth.

You can master power without mastering what power is for.

The politician begins as the servant of politics.

Politics becomes the servant of the politician.

⸻

This does not happen only in Washington.

It may be one of civilization’s favorite mistakes.

We build something because we need it.

Then we learn how to operate it.

Then we learn how to measure it.

Then we learn how to win inside it.

And eventually, without anyone announcing the change, winning inside the system becomes more important than whatever the system was built to do.

A child enters school to learn.

But learning is difficult to see.

So we create grades.

Tests.

Credits.

Degrees.

Rankings.

Admissions.

Credentials.

All useful attempts to measure something real.

Then the measurements acquire consequences.

A number decides which school you enter.

A degree decides which door opens.

A ranking decides where money flows.

And slowly the symbol of education becomes valuable enough to compete with education itself.

The student learns how to get the grade.

The school learns how to raise the score.

The university learns how to climb the ranking.

Everyone can become better at school while nobody asks whether they are becoming better educated.

The machinery works.

That is the problem.

The same thing happens in a newsroom.

The purpose is supposed to be understanding reality.

But understanding is difficult to measure.

Attention is easy.

You can count the click.

You can watch the graph rise.

You can see which headline won.

Which clip traveled.

Which story kept people staring at the screen.

And because attention is necessary for journalism to survive, the measurement makes sense.

Until the measurement starts deciding what journalism is.

A story that helps the public understand something important can lose to a story that makes the public furious.

The dashboard cannot tell the difference.

It only knows which one worked.

And so a machine built to illuminate reality can become extraordinarily sophisticated at capturing attention.

Again:

the machinery works.

A hospital can record every procedure, every diagnosis, every code, every minute, every reimbursement, every liability.

It can become astonishingly precise about the administration of care.

And somewhere inside all that precision is a frightened human being hoping someone will make them well.

That person is the reason the machine exists.

But the farther an institution grows from the human being it was built to serve, the easier it becomes for the machine to mistake itself for the mission.

This is not necessarily because the people inside these systems are cruel.

Often they are doing exactly what the system asks them to do.

That is what makes the problem harder.

A system does not need villains to betray its purpose.

It needs incentives.

The institution may say one thing in its mission statement and another thing through the rewards it distributes every day.

The mission statement is what the institution says it values.

The incentive is what it actually teaches people to value.

We say education is about learning.

Then reward the credential.

We say journalism is about truth.

Then reward attention.

We say politics is about service.

Then reward winning.

Eventually people adapt.

Of course they do.

Every living thing adapts to its environment.

The system says it wants one kind of person.

Its incentives select another.

And after enough time, the institution can become populated by people who are extraordinarily good at satisfying the demands of the machine while becoming increasingly distant from the reason the machine was created.

That is the moment the means becomes the master.

⸻

And that is where Trump returns.

Because perhaps the strangest thing about him is not that he broke American politics.

It is that American politics had already spent decades constructing an environment in which someone like him could become extraordinarily successful.

Attention mattered.

He captured attention.

Television mattered.

He understood television.

Conflict mattered.

He generated conflict.

Identity mattered.

He turned politics into identity.

Loyalty mattered.

He demanded loyalty.

Winning mattered.

He made winning the moral argument.

Trump did not need to master some hidden version of the system.

He mastered the version that was already there.

Which means the disturbing question is not simply:

How did a man like this become president?

It is:

What kind of political environment makes a man like this exceptionally fit?

Every system eventually produces people optimized for what it actually rewards.

So if the winner appalls us, examining the winner is not enough.

We have to examine the prizes.

⸻

This is where the problem becomes larger than politics.

For most of human history, our ability to optimize things was limited by our intelligence, our information, and our tools.

That limit is changing.

Artificial intelligence is, among other things, an enormous expansion of our ability to pursue objectives.

It can help us discover faster, persuade better, predict better, design better, coordinate better, automate better, optimize better.

Which makes the old human question more urgent:

Optimize what?

Optimization is not goodness.

A casino can optimize retention.

A propaganda system can optimize persuasion.

A corporation can optimize profit while destroying the conditions that make its future possible.

Greater capability does not make the objective wiser.

Capability does not contain its own purpose.

Intelligence does not contain its own wisdom.

The machine can become better and better at answering how while the civilization operating it remains confused about why.

This may be one of the central questions of the age.

Not simply whether artificial intelligence will become powerful.

Whether humanity will become clear enough about what power is for before our ability to exercise power becomes almost unimaginable.

Because we have seen this pattern before.

We know how to build mechanisms.

We know how to improve them.

We know how to measure them.

We know how to compete inside them.

We know how to win.

What we are less reliable at remembering is why we started playing.

⸻

And perhaps that is because institutions did not invent this problem.

We did.

We work so that we can live, then become too busy working to live.

We seek money because we want freedom, then surrender our freedom in pursuit of money.

We seek attention because we want to be seen, then reshape ourselves around whatever gets attention.

We develop spiritual practices to encounter God, truth, love, liberation, or reality, then fight over the practices.

We create identities to describe ourselves, then imprison ourselves inside the descriptions.

Again and again, the symbol replaces the thing.

The map replaces the territory.

The metric replaces the meaning.

The means becomes the master.

Civilization magnifies this because civilization is made of human beings.

Our institutions inherit our confusion and scale it.

Increasingly, our technologies automate it.

That is why the question underneath politics is ultimately not political.

It is almost spiritual.

What is all of this for?

What is wealth for?

What is intelligence for?

What is technology for?

What is government for?

What is power for?

If we lose the answer, even improvement can become dangerous.

A civilization can become richer, faster, smarter, more connected, more productive, more technologically capable, and more politically sophisticated while becoming increasingly confused about whether any of it is helping life flourish.

We can become extraordinarily good at moving without knowing where we are going.

Maybe that is the final trap hidden inside progress:

becoming extraordinarily good at achieving things before becoming wise enough to know what is worth achieving.

Trump is one version of the warning.

A man can become very good at politics understood as the acquisition of power while becoming, by another definition, very bad at politics understood as the stewardship of that power toward human flourishing.

The contradiction only sounds strange because we have confused the mechanism with the mission.

The best politician is the worst politician.

The best player can be the worst servant.

And the most dangerous civilization may not be the one whose machinery stops working.

It may be the one whose machinery works perfectly—

after everyone has forgotten what it was built to do.
