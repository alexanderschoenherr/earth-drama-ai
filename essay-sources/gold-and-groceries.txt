Gold and Groceries

What wealth is for in the age of artificial intelligence

Donald Trump has a funny relationship with the word groceries.

During a major economic speech, he stopped for a moment to admire it.

“Groceries.”

An old-fashioned word, he said. A beautiful word. A bag with different things in it.

And I remember hearing this and thinking: yes. Technically. That is what groceries are.

There was something strangely charming about it. Like watching a king discover sandwiches.

To be fair, Trump obviously knows what groceries are. He campaigned relentlessly on grocery prices. Politically, he understands groceries very well.

But most people do not encounter groceries first as a word.

They encounter them as Tuesday.

You need coffee. You are out of eggs. The dog needs food. There is somehow no toothpaste again. You pick up chicken, vegetables, something for breakfast, maybe something unnecessary because you have been alive all week and feel you deserve a cookie.

Then the little screen at the register gives you a number.

That is groceries.

Trump has spent most of his life inside another language of wealth entirely.

Buildings. Property. Deals. Rankings. Brands. Winning. The stock market.

Gold.

A lot of gold.

Gold elevators. Gold rooms. Gold fixtures. Gold lettering. Gold as decoration, gold as atmosphere, gold as a way of making sure nobody accidentally mistakes the room for modest.

There is nothing inherently wrong with gold.

There is nothing wrong with wanting money either. I would personally be delighted to have more of it.

Money can buy safety. Time. Privacy. Healthcare. Pleasure. Options. The ability to say no. The ability to leave. The ability to help somebody. The extraordinary luxury of not thinking about money every seven minutes.

Gold is real wealth.

But gold and groceries are not quite the same relationship to wealth.

That difference keeps bothering me.

Long before Donald Trump became president, America already knew him as a picture of success.

The billionaire.

The tower.

The airplane.

The television boss at the end of the table deciding who had succeeded and who had failed.

The man whose last name was written on the building in letters large enough to be visible from another tax bracket.

Trump did not invent this image.

We already knew how to read it.

Bigger meant better.

Higher meant better.

More expensive meant better.

More famous meant better.

More meant better.

There is a kind of success you can see from across the street.

You can photograph it.

You can rank it.

You can watch the number go up.

The stock market is especially good at this. It gives civilization a scoreboard.

And the scoreboard is real.

The trouble starts when we ask it to tell us everything.

A stock market can rise while somebody is broke. Inflation can fall while groceries still feel expensive. GDP can grow while a particular person feels like their life is shrinking.

All of that can be true at once.

A number describing the whole economy can be accurate and still fail to describe Tuesday.

Trump understands the emotional force of that better than a lot of politicians do.

He knows when people feel forgotten. He knows when they feel cheated. He knows that if somebody says, “I cannot afford my life,” a chart showing that the economy is doing well may not feel like much of an answer.

That does not make his explanation for their pain right.

But recognizing the wound is a real ability.

What I wonder is whether he understands the life around the wound.

Because there is a difference between knowing that grocery prices make people angry and knowing what groceries are doing inside a human life.

Which brings me back to the gold.

Gold is wealth that stays wealth.

That is part of the magic.

You can store it. Own it. Measure it. Trade it. Display it. Put it in a vault. Put it in the wall. Apparently put it in the bathroom.

It remains.

Groceries are terrible at this.

Groceries refuse to stay wealth.

You bring them home and they start disappearing immediately.

The strawberries last four minutes.

The coffee lasts a week.

Someone eats the chicken.

Someone drops an egg.

Something grows fur in the back of the refrigerator and eventually becomes a different form of life entirely.

Then you have to go buy groceries again.

From the point of view of accumulation, groceries are a disaster.

But that is exactly what makes them interesting.

Groceries are wealth crossing the border into life.

Money becomes bread.

Money becomes coffee.

Money becomes peaches.

Money becomes dinner.

Money becomes a body that is no longer hungry.

And then the wealth is gone.

Except it is not exactly gone.

It has become something else.

That seems worth remembering whenever we talk about prosperity.

Because eventually wealth has to become something.

Why do we want it?

Maybe we want security.

Maybe we want freedom.

Maybe we want beauty, or status, or time, or a house, or a business, or enough money to stop checking the balance before we order what we actually want.

Sometimes we want money because money feels good.

Sometimes we want more because somebody else has more.

Sometimes there is no amount that produces the feeling we thought the amount was going to produce.

But underneath all of that is a very simple question:

What is this for?

It is surprisingly easy for a system to forget.

The company needs to grow. The portfolio needs a return. The career needs to advance. The number needs to go up.

At some point, a thing built to serve human life can quietly become the thing human life is serving.

That seems backwards.

People do not exist so the economy can have a good quarter.

We built economies because human beings needed ways to trade, save, coordinate, build, share risk, create abundance, make plans, and keep going.

And this is where a strange little essay about Donald Trump talking about groceries becomes, unfortunately, much larger.

Because we are entering a period in which human beings may become vastly more capable of getting what they want.

Artificial intelligence is already becoming another layer of human capability.

The thing that interests me most about it is not whether it becomes conscious, steals every job, or writes the next Hamlet.

It is a simpler question.

What happens when we become much better at pursuing what we already value?

AI is not arriving in an empty civilization.

It is arriving here.

Into the civilization we already made.

Into our companies, governments, markets, ambitions, institutions, desires, and definitions of success.

We have spent thousands of years wanting things.

Wealth. Security. Power. Beauty. Knowledge. Revenge. Comfort. Freedom. Love. Recognition.

And now we are building tools that may give wanting itself more leverage.

That is thrilling.

It is also a little terrifying.

Not because desire is bad.

Desire built civilization.

But becoming more capable does not automatically make us wiser about where all that capability should go.

If you become spectacularly good at moving toward the wrong thing, you have not solved the problem.

You have just arrived faster.

And there is no single Humanity sitting at a desk deciding what the machine should do.

Governments want things. Companies want things. Artists want things. Militaries want things. Families want things. Investors want things. Criminals want things. People trying to save the world want things. People trying to make the quarterly number want things.

The future will be built out of all of that.

Which means the question of what we call success is about to matter even more.

Capability gives values reach.

It lets preferences become systems.

And if a civilization becomes extraordinarily powerful while remaining confused about what prosperity is for, the confusion does not disappear.

It becomes infrastructure.

This may be what Donald Trump accidentally helps illuminate.

Not because Trump is America.

He isn’t.

Not because everything he values is wrong.

It isn’t.

But he is an unusually visible expression of one way of understanding success.

The tower.

The name.

The market.

The number.

The gold.

And then, every once in a while:

groceries.

The funny little word.

The bag with different things in it.

I keep coming back to that bag.

Because somewhere tonight somebody is carrying one home.

Maybe there is nothing profound about it.

Milk.

Coffee.

Chicken.

Some vegetables they fully intend to eat before they go bad this time.

A dessert they did not need.

Food for the dog.

Something somebody else in the house asked for.

They put it on the counter.

They make dinner.

People eat.

They talk.

Maybe they fight.

Maybe somebody laughs with food in their mouth.

Maybe somebody is alone and eats standing over the sink.

Tomorrow the bag goes in the trash or gets folded into a drawer containing thirty-seven other bags.

No wealth remains on display.

Nothing has appreciated.

No bell rings.

No television network reports the result.

The groceries have failed completely as gold.

They have become life.
