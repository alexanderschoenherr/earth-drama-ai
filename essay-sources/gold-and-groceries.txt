Gold and Groceries

A fable about Donald Trump, the price of food, and what America has mistaken for wealth.

There was once a very rich King who had a peculiar relationship with groceries.

He knew what groceries were.

This is worth establishing early. The King was not wandering through the palace asking why citizens carried onions in sacks. He understood bread, butter, apples, eggs, meat, and flour. He knew these things could be placed together in a bag and carried home.

What fascinated him was the word.

Groceries.

He would pause before it. The word seemed to delight him. It was old-fashioned, he said. Beautiful. Strange and simple. A bag with different things in it.

Most people in the kingdom had never considered groceries beautiful. They considered them breakfast. Or dinner. Or, increasingly, the thing at the market they picked up, learned the price of, and placed back.

But the King did not begin as a King.

In this kingdom, kings were chosen. This did not eliminate the usual problems with kings, but it gave the people a scheduled opportunity to participate in them.

Before seeking the crown, the future King was a famous builder and merchant. His name appeared on towers, inns, halls, and nearly anything else that could be made taller by attaching large letters to it. His father had been one of the richest builders in the kingdom and gave his son land, buildings, money, introductions, and the kind of family name that entered certain rooms several minutes before the person carrying it.

There was nothing uniquely wicked about this. Parents have always tried to give their children advantages. Most would give them more if they could. But circumstances teach. So does being spared certain circumstances.

The future King never had to learn through necessity what happened when there simply was not enough. He did not have to discover that sixty coins could change the emotional temperature of an entire week.

He learned other things. He learned where to place his name on a building so it could be seen from the road. At banquets, he noticed which insult made the table laugh and which one made it go quiet. Before crowds, he could feel when people wanted hope and when they wanted an enemy.

Some people mistook what he did not know for stupidity. That was a mistake.

When he decided to seek the crown, he traveled through parts of the kingdom where workshops stood empty and young people left without returning. The capital still spoke of progress, but progress had developed a habit of arriving somewhere else.

People felt forgotten. Many felt mocked as well. They heard nobles and scholars describe them as backward, ignorant, embarrassing, or inconvenient. The court remembered that they existed when it needed their marks, their labor, or their sons for war.

The future King listened. Perhaps not to every detail. But he listened for the wound.

One afternoon, near a crowded marketplace, a woman told him that groceries cost too much. She stood beside a basket containing bread, onions, apples, and a small piece of cheese. She counted her coins. Then she removed the cheese.

“Groceries,” the future King repeated.

A few people nearby repeated it too.

Other candidates spoke of the general rise in prices. They summoned learned advisers and unfurled parchments covered in columns. The future King said groceries, and everyone could see the market stall, the coins in their hands, and the thing they had placed back.

He began saying it everywhere. The old rulers had made groceries too expensive. His enemies had destroyed groceries. Give him the crown and groceries would become affordable again.

He had discovered groceries. Not the food, exactly. The politics.

One of his learned opponents eventually came to the marketplace with encouraging news. The rate at which prices were increasing had slowed, he explained.

He was correct.

The woman looked down at her basket. The cheese was still not in it.

The future King did not arrive with a better calculation. He arrived with culprits.

They had cheated you, he told the people. They had forgotten you. They laughed at you because they believed they were better than you.

“I will fight them.”

The people’s wounds had many causes, and some of the people he blamed had little to do with them. But the wounds were real. Some people believed everything he said. Others believed only half and considered that more than they believed from anyone else. Some wanted cheaper food or the return of work. Others wanted the capital humiliated as thoroughly as they believed it had humiliated them.

His opponents frequently laughed at him. He was very good at making the laughter sound as though it were directed at everyone standing beside him.

On the appointed day, the people made their marks. When the marks were counted, the builder became the King.

Groceries did not win him the crown by themselves. Crowns do not rest on a single word.

But groceries helped.

The new King entered the People’s Palace, where generations of Kings had lived temporarily on behalf of the kingdom. He looked around.

The first thing he noticed was how much of it was not gold.

The People’s Palace was not an ugly place. It had tall windows, carved doors, old stone, polished wood, and portraits of former Kings trying to look as though they had always known what to do.

But much of it was made from materials that had failed to become gold.

The King summoned artisans. Gold appeared around the fireplace, then along the walls, then around the doors. Small golden objects gathered on tables. Larger golden objects arrived to keep them company.

The palace belonged to the people, although almost none of them were permitted inside. Each King lived there temporarily and was allowed to leave some evidence of his taste. This King wanted no future visitor to wonder what he liked.

In the center of the capital stood the Great Exchange. Merchants gathered there to buy and sell interests in ships, mines, cargo, land, and trading ventures. Each day, scribes recorded what those interests were worth and carried the numbers throughout the kingdom.

The Exchange mattered. When the value of a fleet rose, the people who owned part of it became richer. They could sell their interests, purchase new ships, or hire more workers. Fortunes were genuinely made there. Fortunes were also lost.

One afternoon, while an artisan was applying a thin layer of gold around a doorway, a messenger arrived.

The Exchange was up.

The King smiled. “Excellent,” he said, and told the artisan to continue.

Among the people nearest to the King was a woman known as the Harpist. She was called that because she played the harp.

When the King wanted calm, she played calmly. When he wanted triumph, she played triumphantly. When silence threatened to fill with something unpleasant, she could usually find a more agreeable sound. She sat close enough to learn the weather of the room.

Messages arrived throughout the day: letters from nobles, reports from distant towns, notes from merchants, accounts of royal gatherings, and stories about what people were saying. The Harpist began helping decide which should be placed near the King.

She did not forge them. A village really had thanked the King for repairing a bridge. A noble really had praised his speech. A crowd really had cheered.

The Harpist arranged several such messages on a small table beside him. The others remained in a larger pile farther away. Nothing in that pile had been forbidden. It had simply failed to reach the smaller table.

Each morning, royal scribes also delivered the Book of Tidings, assembled from reports sent by spies, scouts, ambassadors, generals, and watchmen. One morning it brought news of soldiers moving near a border, a failing harvest, and a secret quarrel between two foreign rulers.

The King had portions read aloud. He listened, asked several questions, and gave an instruction to a general. Then the Book was closed and carried away.

The Harpist’s table remained beside him. On it was an admiring letter from a noble, an account of a successful gathering, and a report that people in a distant town had called him the greatest King who had ever lived.

The Book told him what the world was doing. The table told him what the world was saying about him.

The palace employed Heralds as well. The Harpist helped pleasant information travel inward. The Heralds helped the King’s preferred information travel out.

They stood in public squares and rode from town to town announcing the King’s victories, the rise of the Great Exchange, the defeat of his enemies, and the gratitude of his people. They carried trumpets. This was useful because a claim that sounded uncertain when spoken quietly could acquire considerable confidence after a trumpet.

At a gathering in the capital, the King announced that the kingdom had never been richer.

The crowd cheered. Some cheered because their interests on the Exchange had risen. Some cheered because they loved the King. Others cheered because everyone around them was cheering and it seemed unfriendly not to.

The Heralds rode out. In every town they announced that the people of the capital had roared with joy upon hearing how prosperous the kingdom had become.

A messenger carried an account of the gathering back to the palace. The people had cheered wildly, the report said. They understood the King’s success. They knew the kingdom had never been richer.

The Harpist placed the message on the small table.

“The people understand what we are doing,” the King said.

At the next gathering, he told an even larger crowd that no kingdom in the history of the world had ever been as prosperous as theirs. The crowd cheered again. The Heralds lifted their trumpets. Another messenger began riding toward the palace.

No one had forged a message. No one had invented a cheer.

The Harpist began to play something triumphant.

The woman with the basket did not hear the harp. She was back at the marketplace.

Bread still cost more than she wanted it to. So did the onions. The cheese had become a little smaller, although its price had shown no similar modesty.

She counted her coins. Once again, the cheese remained behind.

A Herald entered the square and blew his trumpet. The kingdom, he announced, had never been more prosperous.

The woman looked at her basket. “Groceries still cost too much,” she said. Other people said it too.

Before the King had taken power, those words had been evidence that the old rulers had failed. Now the same words implicated him.

The complaint about affordability, the King said, had been invented by his enemies. They wanted people to feel poor because they could not bear to admit how successful the kingdom had become.

The people looked at their coins. The King looked at the latest message from the capital.

The Exchange was up.

Not everyone owned part of a ship, mine, or trading venture.

Everyone ate.

The kingdom also maintained a Royal Counting House. Its officials counted how many people were working, what they were paid, what common goods cost, and whether conditions were improving. Early estimates sometimes changed when reports arrived from distant regions. The purpose of the Counting House was to become more accurate as more became known.

At its head was the Keeper of the Numbers. She was supposed to count what was happening.

One morning, the Counting House completed a discouraging report. Fewer people had found work than previously believed. Several earlier estimates had been too hopeful. Wages in parts of the kingdom had not risen enough to meet the cost of ordinary life.

The Keeper released the numbers.

The King said the report had been corrupted to embarrass him. The Keeper, he declared, had allowed his enemies to enter the Counting House.

The Keeper said that the numbers were the numbers.

The King removed her.

The numbers did not improve. But everyone else in the palace learned something.

Several days later, another messenger approached the King’s chamber carrying an unpleasant report. He stopped outside the door. From within, he could hear the Harpist playing.

The messenger looked down at the parchment. He scratched out had fallen sharply and replaced it with had not risen as much as hoped. He changed many families to some families. He removed an entire sentence concerning the price of flour.

Near the bottom was one final piece of news. He left that sentence exactly as it was.

Then he entered the chamber, bowed, and read the revised report.

The Exchange was up.

The Harpist continued playing.

Outside the palace, many people remained devoted to the King. This confused his opponents, some of whom concluded that anyone who still supported him must be stupid, dishonest, or mad.

One evening, a cartwright overheard a visiting clerk joke that only fools still believed the King. The cartwright’s shop had once employed twelve people and now employed three. He did not believe everything the King said. But he remembered years in which nobody from the royal court had spoken about his town at all. Then the King had arrived, named the abandoned workshops, and spoken about humiliation.

The cartwright turned from his meal and defended the King more strongly than he had intended.

The King was very good at recognizing the shape of the wound. Whether he understood the life surrounding it was less clear.

It would also be unfair to claim that the King taught the kingdom to worship wealth. The kingdom had been practicing for years.

Children were taken to halls filled with portraits of rich merchants. Minstrels sang about their estates. People who would never be invited inside learned the number of rooms and the size of the gardens. A merchant who became rich selling wool might soon be asked for his views on war, education, poetry, and the proper arrangement of the human soul.

He usually had views.

Wealth produced fame, and fame acquired the sound of authority. By the time the builder sought the crown, the kingdom had already taught its people to see his towers and estates as the outward signs of success.

The King did not invent that lesson. He had learned it very well.

Now the People’s Palace shone above the capital.

It looked successful.

Up close, success required maintenance.

The gold around the palace doors did not rise from the earth, flatten itself into delicate sheets, and climb onto the walls. An artisan put it there.

She arrived each morning carrying her tools and a small bundle of food. At midday, she stopped, sat beneath a window, and ate a piece of bread with an apple.

Until she had finished, the gold waited.

Before reaching her hands, it had been mined, guarded, beaten into sheets, and carried along roads maintained by workers who would never enter the palace. The miners ate. The drivers ate. The horses ate grain.

Gold was valuable. It could be stored, exchanged, saved through a good year and spent during a bad one. It could purchase groceries. It could not become them by itself.

At night, the King ate dinner in a golden room. Bread, meat, vegetables, fruit, and wine arrived from farms, kitchens, roads, and hands he did not know. His wealth paid for it.

If the farmers disappeared, the kitchen would empty. If the workers disappeared, the palace would darken. The gold would remain exactly where it was: beautiful, valuable, and no help at dinner.

While artisans gilded the palace and scribes recorded the Exchange, scholars, philosophers, artificers, and engineers were constructing something the kingdom had never seen before.

They called it the Great Engine.

People said it might one day answer questions, read more books than any scholar, reason, write, compose music, design buildings, discover medicines, and create things no human being had imagined. The claims sounded impossible. Then some of them stopped sounding impossible.

Farmers wondered whether it could predict frost. Physicians wondered whether it could discover cures. Merchants imagined greater trade, generals imagined victory, artists imagined new beauty, and rulers imagined greater power.

The people argued endlessly about what the Engine might do. Much less was said about what all that power should serve.

In the distance, the People’s Palace shone above the capital. Below it, the markets opened each morning.

The Great Engine waited for instructions.

There is one problem with this fable.

Almost none of what makes it strange is invented.

The scenes are mine. The machinery is not.

The King is Donald Trump. The People’s Palace is the White House. The Great Exchange is the stock market. The Royal Counting House is the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the Keeper of the Numbers is Erika McEntarfer, whom Trump removed after the agency released discouraging employment numbers and major revisions to earlier estimates.

The Book of Tidings is the President’s Daily Brief and the intelligence system surrounding it. The Heralds are a composite of political surrogates, friendly media figures, and officials who carry Trump’s preferred account of reality outward. The Great Engine is artificial intelligence.

The gold is gold. Trump has covered parts of the White House, especially the Oval Office, with it. The groceries are groceries. Trump has repeatedly paused over the word, calling it old-fashioned and beautiful and describing it as something simple and strange: a bag containing different things.

The Harpist is Natalie Harp. Her surname really is Harp. As far as I know, she does not play an actual harp for Trump. Reality stopped one step short. I supplied the harp. Harp became known for accompanying him with a portable printer and supplying him with articles, posts, and messages.

The woman at the market, the cartwright, the visiting clerk, and the messenger with the parchment are mine. The names, objects, institutions, and public events beneath the fable are real. Those are the facts. The fable is what I am making of them.

Harp’s surname proves nothing cosmic. Her printer does not prove that everything she gives Trump is flattering or false. Gold decoration does not reveal a man’s soul, and I cannot see inside Trump’s private mind. I do not want to cheat to make the fable work. The facts are strange enough.

The political danger does not depend on the symbolism. When a ruler punishes an institution for producing numbers he dislikes, the other messengers learn without being ordered. The question is not whether a leader receives information. It is what survives inside the room.

The cartwright is mine, but the wound is not. Trump’s ability to recognize humiliation is real political intelligence. It does not make the story he attaches to that pain true. He may understand some of his supporters’ wounds better than he understands their lives.

Trump is not America. America is too large and contradictory to fit inside one man. But Trump is American too. He makes one American hierarchy of value unusually visible: inherited wealth as self-creation, celebrity as authority, branding as reality, winning as virtue, the stock market as universal prosperity, and gold as success.

He did not fall from the sky carrying these ideas. America had been practicing them: celebrating enormous wealth, turning fortune into fame, and asking the famous what they thought about everything else.

America spent decades teaching people that Donald Trump looked like success. Then it elected the man who looked exactly like what it had taught them to admire.

None of this absolves Trump. It asks what America believed wealth proved—and what wealth was for.

Gold has value. So do markets, property, trade, and growth. They can create shelter, medicine, art, freedom, and time. The mistake is forgetting what wealth exists to serve.

By this point, reality appears to be showing off: Trump, gold, groceries, the People’s Palace, the Keeper of the Numbers, and a woman named Harp carrying a portable printer into the room.

It feels written.

I do not know whether history has a writer. Maybe human beings find symbols in events because that is one of the ways we make sense of what happens. Maybe history contains an order I do not understand. Maybe those possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

I do not know.

A mirror does not have to be divinely placed to show you your face.

And now, while the mirror is still in front of us, we are building the Great Engine.

Artificial intelligence may become very good at producing whatever our institutions already reward.

Trump is the mirror. The Great Engine is the amplifier.

The question is not only what artificial intelligence will become capable of doing. It is what we will ask it to serve. A civilization still confused about what wealth is for is acquiring extraordinary power to make more of whatever it chooses.

By late afternoon, the marketplace was beginning to close.

The woman stood before the final stall with the coins she had left. She chose bread, onions, apples, and a small piece of cheese.

She counted.

Then she placed the cheese back.

Above the town, the People’s Palace caught the lowering sun. Its gold could be seen from a great distance.

No Herald announced what the woman had done. No trumpet sounded.

She placed the remaining groceries in her basket.

Then she carried them home.
