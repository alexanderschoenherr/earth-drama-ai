Perfect

How to make peace with a world you still want to change.

I told someone I thought the universe was perfect.

They thought it was the complete opposite.

I can understand that. Look around. There are some fairly substantial complaints to be made.

But calling the universe perfect doesn’t mean I approve of everything happening. I’m looking at how it all came to be.

Look at a planet.

Its shape, its surface, its atmosphere—whatever you find there came out of how it formed and what happened to it. You wouldn’t look at a crater and think the planet should have made better choices.

Something hit it.

Of course there’s a crater.

Now look at a person.

This gets considerably harder.

Because a person can speak. They can lie. They can know they’re hurting someone and keep doing it. They can appear to be standing there, freely selecting exactly how terrible they would like to be.

But where did that person come from?

They didn’t choose their parents. Their body. Where they were born. The world they arrived into. Whether the people around them were tender, frightened, violent, available, absent.

Before they knew there was anything to choose, life was already happening to them. They were learning what love felt like. What danger looked like. What got rewarded. What had to be hidden. Whether other people could be trusted.

And when they began making choices, they were making them as the person all of this was helping them become. Even their sense of what was possible, desirable, or frightening had been taking shape.

And the people teaching them had arrived somewhere in the middle of their own stories.

Keep going backward.

Nobody’s parents invented themselves either.

At some point, you are no longer looking at an isolated person who somehow decided to become this. You are looking at a life emerging from other lives, from circumstances, from a world that was already underway.

We all woke up inside something.

Here is your body. Here is your family. Here is your century. Here are some things you will spend forty years discovering you learned before you could speak.

Good luck.

And then we look at each other and ask why we aren’t different.

Sometimes that is a useful question. But sometimes what we mean is: why didn’t you somehow arrive here from a completely different existence?

“I would never be like that.”

With the life you’ve had, perhaps you wouldn’t.

But give yourself their entire beginning. Their body, their experiences, everything they encountered and everything they never encountered. Take away everything your own life gave you.

Even the part of you saying “I would never” came from your life.

Who, exactly, are you imagining would be left?

This is where something softens for me.

I can still see what someone is doing. I can still want it stopped. But I’m no longer looking at a creature who created itself out of nothing and inexplicably chose the worst available design.

I’m looking at someone who became this way.

And this isn’t only about people.

Look at anything.

The chair. The tree. The building across the street. The person inside it. Your own hand. The thought you’re having about all of this.

None of it began with your noticing it. Follow what brought it here, and what brought that here, and you are reaching backward toward the beginning of the universe.

And now here it is.

All of this.

The universe didn’t stop happening somewhere in the distant past and leave us to do something unrelated. We are inside its happening. This moment is its history arriving as a moment.

That is the perfection I’m trying to describe: the feeling of seeing this moment make sense within everything that brought it here.

I don’t mean a world with nothing painful in it.

For a moment, I stop looking at reality as though it has made an administrative error.

Of course.

Of course this is what has emerged from what happened.

I don’t have to know every detail to recognize that there were details. I don’t have to approve of the result to see that it belongs to the existence that produced it.

I can still wish things were different. But instead of getting stuck on “This shouldn’t be here,” I can begin to see how it got here. The person, the damage, the situation—something that felt inexplicable begins to make sense.

And there is peace in that.

The kind where I can finally put down the demand that this moment have a different past.

It has this one.

So do I.

My mistakes came from somewhere too. So did the things I didn’t understand until afterward. I can look at who I was without expecting him to know what I learned by living through those mistakes.

There can be grief in that. There can be responsibility. But there can also be some mercy.

If everything comes out of what happens to it, what happens now matters.

What I do matters.

Understanding someone can change how I treat them. How I treat them can become part of what happens to them. A boundary, a kindness, a refusal, a conversation—these enter the same unfolding that brought us both here.

Why would seeing causes make me powerless?

I’m involved in what happens next.

My desire to change something is here too. My anger at cruelty. My wish to help. I don’t have to remove those from the picture to make the universe perfect. They are part of the picture.

A person tending a wound belongs to the same universe as the wound.

Perfect doesn’t mean finished.

I can begin with what is actually here.

I can stop demanding a different starting point. Stop imagining that hatred supplies an explanation. Stop treating myself and everyone else as though we arrived without origins.

I can look.

Here is what has happened.

Here is what it has become.

And here we are, becoming part of what happens to it.
