How Democrats Should Fight Trump

The liar can say anything. The truth-teller must learn to do everything else.

Why Aren’t They Fighting?

This is what a lot of people keep asking about the Democrats.

Trump or one of his Republicans comes out with some spectacular lie—something hateful, ridiculous, or simply detached from observable reality—and the Democratic response sounds like someone has assigned them a dissertation on whether the sky is blue.

“Well, actually, according to the available evidence—”

And the evidence matters. Obviously. But by the time they finish explaining it, Trump has declared the evidence a hoax, awarded himself a doctorate, blamed the university on illegal immigrants, and moved on to something else.

Then the Democrats release a statement saying they are deeply concerned.

I don’t think most people who say Democrats need to “fight harder” are literally asking them to yell more. They are not sitting at home thinking, I would really support this party if Chuck Schumer would just increase his volume by 30 percent.

They are asking to feel that someone besides Trump is capable of making something happen.

Trump acts like he has an intention. He tells you what the world is, who ruined it, what he is going to do, and what victory will look like.

Democrats too often sound like they have been assigned to explain why several parts of his description are inaccurate.

He acts like the author.

They act like fact-checkers in the back of his book.

That, I think, is the deeper complaint hiding inside “Why aren’t they fighting?”

People don’t only want a more accurate description of the danger. They want direction. Some evidence of will. Some reason to believe the future is not simply whatever Trump announces next while everyone else stands around explaining why it violates longstanding norms.

And Democrats are not innocent truth machines. They spin. They evade. They protect donors, institutions, and themselves. Sometimes they lie.

But there is still a real asymmetry between an opposition that feels some obligation to remain answerable to evidence and a leader who has discovered that he can say almost anything with enormous confidence and make the rest of the country deal with it.

The liar has more freedom.

A person trying to tell the truth has to remain somewhere near what actually happened. There are documents. Numbers. Witnesses. Previous statements. Uncertainty. New information that may arrive tomorrow and force a correction.

The liar can use any of those things when they are convenient.

He can also invent the motive, the enemy, the victory, the conspiracy, the statistic, the memory, and the version of himself who was right all along.

Reality gives the truth-teller a limited field of possible claims.

The liar gets the field, the parking lot, and several adjacent properties.

That freedom gives him more possible performances. He can be absolutely certain about something that is not certain. He can simplify what is not simple. He can promise what no honest person could promise. He can change the story the moment the old one becomes inconvenient and never visibly experience the contradiction.

And strangely, all of this can make him seem more authentic.

I kept trying to understand that part.

How can someone who lies so constantly make people feel that he is the one telling the truth?

Then I realized that truth and conviction do not reach us in the same way.

Truth usually has to be established. You need evidence, context, knowledge, or enough trust in somebody who has done the work.

Conviction can be felt immediately.

You can hear hesitation. You can see when someone seems embarrassed by his own sentence. You can feel whether the speaker is guarded, calculated, divided, or completely behind what he is saying.

Research on political authenticity has found that people often experience a politician as authentic when he seems consistent, immediate, and directly present in his own words. None of that guarantees factual accuracy.

A person can be completely faithful to himself while being completely unfaithful to reality.

This is one of Trump’s strangest powers.

He often seems truthful because he seems completely committed to what he wants to be true.

Watching him, I started to realize that he really seems to live like he is writing his own story.

He won.

Everyone agrees with him.

The event was the greatest success anyone has ever seen.

The institution investigating him is corrupt.

The person contradicting him is suddenly a traitor, a loser, a lunatic, or somebody nobody has ever heard of.

It doesn’t seem to matter whether the story began in reality. It is the story he needs, so he starts speaking it as reality.

And then something actually does happen.

His supporters repeat it. Republican officials rearrange themselves around it. Journalists investigate it. Opponents denounce it. Institutions have to decide how to respond.

The claim may remain false while becoming politically consequential.

Trump cannot literally announce physical reality into submission. If a bridge collapses, calling it the strongest bridge in history will not put it back in the air.

But politics is partly made from things language can affect: attention, expectation, legitimacy, fear, identity, repetition, authority.

Who appears to have won?

Who appears impossible to resist?

Who counts as an enemy?

What now counts as an emergency?

What can a president do today that would have shocked people a year ago?

Trump does not merely lie about the story.

He tries to write the story everyone else has to live inside.

And his opponents often help him.

They repeat his language. They accept his subject. They answer the question he chose. They spend the day disproving the world he invented instead of telling people what he is doing, what is actually happening, and what they intend to make happen instead.

Trump remains the protagonist even in the story of resistance to Trump.

This is where his opponents make their most important mistake.

They have mistaken being constrained by truth for being condemned to reaction.

They behave as though caring about reality permits them only to correct, qualify, object, warn, document, and wait.

It doesn’t.

Truth limits what you may claim.

Good.

Keep the limit.

But it does not require you to surrender authorship, conviction, humor, simplicity, repetition, moral force, strategy, or action.

⸻

Stop Letting Trump Write the Story

The first thing the truth-teller has to learn is to stop letting the liar decide what the conversation is about.

Trump says something insane and suddenly everyone who opposes him has been handed an assignment.

Was the election stolen?

Is this judge corrupt?

Are these people secretly destroying America?

Is this institution controlled by whatever frightening ideological word happens to be useful this week?

Now everyone gets to work.

The journalists investigate. The experts produce numbers. Democrats explain the law. Someone makes a graphic. Four days later, we have assembled a very strong case proving that the world Trump invented on Tuesday does not, in fact, exist.

Congratulations.

We have also spent four days talking about his world.

Sometimes a lie has to be corrected. If somebody is being endangered, falsely accused, stripped of rights, or governed according to false information, the facts matter.

But a political lie is often doing more than communicating false information.

It is trying to make something happen.

Maybe it is creating an enemy.

Maybe it is preparing people to accept an action that would otherwise seem cruel.

Maybe it is transferring blame away from the people who actually have power.

Maybe it is discrediting an institution before that institution restrains him.

Maybe it is simply placing something enormous and stupid in the middle of the room so nobody notices what is happening behind it.

So before asking only, “Is this true?” Trump’s opponents need another reflex:

What is this statement doing?

That question moves you from correction into interpretation.

Suppose Trump calls an institution investigating him corrupt.

You may need to establish whether the accusation is true. But the larger pattern may be that he repeatedly describes restraints on his own power as illegitimate attacks.

Now the public has more than a fact-check.

It has a pattern.

Different target. Same move.

Or suppose a vulnerable population is suddenly identified as the source of some national problem.

You can debate every statistic inside the claim.

But you should also ask why these people are the story right now. Who actually has power over the problem? Who benefits from everybody looking down instead of up? What action becomes easier once the public is frightened enough?

You do not need to pretend you can read Trump’s mind. Sometimes you can identify the effect of a message more confidently than its private intention.

Say that honestly.

Truthful framing is not mind-reading.

It is refusing to surrender interpretation.

Democrats surrender interpretation constantly.

Republicans introduce the noun, choose the villain, define the stakes, and then invite Democrats onto television to argue over the adjective.

You can win the factual dispute and still lose the story.

And Democrats make the problem worse by treating every controversy as though history restarted that morning.

New scandal.

New statement.

New panel discussion.

New reason this particular thing is unprecedented.

But people don’t need seven hundred unrelated reasons to be alarmed. They need help seeing the few patterns connecting them.

Trump understands this.

The details change constantly, but the emotional story is remarkably stable.

He is strong. His enemies are corrupt. You have been betrayed. They are coming for you. He is the only person willing to fight.

If something good happens, he caused it.

If something bad happens, somebody else did.

Democrats often do the reverse. They have thousands of facts and no story strong enough to hold them together.

Bernie Sanders, whatever one thinks of every part of his politics, demonstrates the power of doing the opposite. He has spent years returning to a small number of governing ideas—concentrated wealth, oligarchy, working-class power—and connecting new events back to them. No single frame explains everything. But people know what he thinks is happening.

That matters.

A political movement should be able to tell people the few truths that new events keep revealing.

And then it should keep telling them.

Political professionals become bored with repetition much faster than ordinary people become familiar with a message.

“They already said that.”

“Everyone knows.”

No.

Everyone in your group chat knows.

Most of the country was doing something else.

If an idea is important enough to organize public understanding around, it should survive more than one news cycle.

This is not propaganda.

Propaganda starts with the desired belief and bends reality around it.

Truthful storytelling starts with reality and does the work reality cannot do by itself: selection, connection, interpretation.

Facts do not arrive with little labels explaining which ones matter most.

A citizen looking at years of executive orders, court cases, speeches, firings, investigations, appointments, economic decisions, scandals, threats, and reversals should not be expected to independently assemble the governing pattern while making dinner.

That is part of what political leadership is for.

Tell people what connects the events.

Tell them often enough that they can begin recognizing the pattern themselves.

A political idea has not fully entered public life because a senator said it once on cable news.

It has entered public life when somebody can explain it to his mother.

When a worker can say it at lunch.

When somebody sees another event and thinks:

Oh.

He is doing that thing again.

⸻

Truth Has to Sound Like Truth

But even the truer story has to sound like somebody believes it.

A person can be factually right and still sound weak.

A person can be completely wrong and sound like the only person in the room who knows what is going on.

We like to imagine that human beings can sense truth directly.

Sometimes maybe we can.

A lot of the time, what we are sensing is conviction.

I have noticed this outside politics too. There are moments when somebody says something with enough certainty and alignment that everyone else almost seems to go quiet around it. The room changes.

It does not mean the person is right.

It means the person is not divided.

Trump lives this.

He says things with the emotional force of someone reporting a fact directly from God even when the fact apparently arrived three seconds earlier from Donald Trump.

There is no visible struggle.

No “on the other hand.”

No moment where you watch him wondering whether the evidence quite supports what he is saying.

That can look like honesty, especially in a culture where so much public language sounds processed, pre-approved, sterilized, and frightened of itself.

People say:

“At least he tells you what he thinks.”

I understand why that feels meaningful.

There is something relieving about encountering somebody who appears to be speaking from somewhere inside himself instead of from a document that passed through twelve people and a lawyer.

The problem is that being faithful to yourself is not the same thing as being faithful to reality.

Trump can seem authentic because he appears completely aligned with Trump.

That does not make him aligned with the world.

His opponents often make the opposite mistake.

Because they are trying not to overstate, they become visibly uncertain about everything.

“We are continuing to review the available information.”

“There are still unanswered questions.”

“It would be premature to draw conclusions.”

Sometimes that is exactly what an honest person should say.

The problem is when uncertainty about one part of reality spreads into the whole message.

Maybe you do not know exactly why an official did something.

You may still know what the action does.

Maybe you do not know precisely how many people will be harmed.

You may still know which principle is being violated.

Maybe you do not know what a court will ultimately decide.

You can still know what you intend to defend.

There is a difference between saying:

“We do not know yet.”

and sounding like:

“We do not know what we believe.”

Democrats need to locate uncertainty where it actually belongs instead of pouring it over the entire reality like gravy.

Say what you do not know.

Then say what you do.

This action gives the president a power he should not have.

These people are being harmed.

This official made this decision.

We are challenging it.

That is not fake certainty.

It is precision.

And if you believe something is morally serious, it should occasionally sound like you believe it is morally serious.

If somebody uses public power to punish a personal enemy, say that.

If somebody lies to frighten people into accepting cruelty, say that.

Accuracy does not require emotional anesthesia.

Anger can be truthful.

Humor can be truthful.

Grief can be truthful.

Love can be truthful.

Emotion becomes dishonest when it replaces reality, not when it helps people understand what reality means.

For some reason, Democrats often seem willing to concede the entire emotional spectrum to Trump except “grave concern.”

Trump gets anger.

Trump gets ridicule.

Trump gets pride.

Trump gets defiance.

Trump gets to be funny.

His opponents get an extremely serious podium.

Why?

There is nothing inherently Republican about having a pulse.

But the answer is not manufactured passion either.

People can sense fake authenticity too.

The goal is simpler and harder: know what you actually believe. Know what you do not know. Know what you refuse. Know what you intend.

Then stop hiding it underneath language designed mainly to survive criticism.

A truthful leader also has a freedom Trump rarely uses.

He can say:

I was wrong.

Here is the correction.

That does not have to destroy authority. It can demonstrate that the person’s loyalty is to reality rather than his own infallibility.

The goal is not to become as certain as Trump.

It is to become as aligned as he appears to be, but aligned with something more reliable than appetite.

⸻

Show People Where the Power Is

Then you have to bring all of this down to earth.

A lot of Democratic language is technically correct and emotionally weightless.

“Democratic institutions.”

“The rule of law.”

“Executive overreach.”

“Threats to institutional independence.”

These things matter enormously.

They can also sound like material from the least exciting hour of a graduate seminar.

Meanwhile, people are trying to figure out whether they can afford rent. Whether their insurance will cover something. Whether their job is disappearing. Whether the government can take something from them and there is anyone left who can say no.

That does not mean people only care about grocery prices.

It means political language has to complete the journey from the institution to the human being.

Because that is where power eventually goes anyway.

If the president weakens an independent institution, ask what becomes easier because that institution is weaker.

Who gains the ability to do something?

Who loses the ability to stop them?

If the Justice Department becomes personally loyal to the president, “institutional independence” describes the problem.

So does:

Whether the government investigates you should depend on evidence, not whether the president likes you.

Now you can feel it.

Democracy suffers from the same abstraction.

Democrats say Trump is a threat to democracy so often that democracy can begin to sound like the name of a very important building everyone agrees should remain open.

But democracy is not a monument.

It is a distribution of power.

It means you can remove leaders who abuse you.

It means a president cannot decide your vote matters only when he likes the result.

It means public officials work for the public, not the other way around.

You do not lose freedom only when somebody officially announces that you are no longer free.

You can also lose it when everyone quietly learns the cost of saying no.

So translate the abstraction.

Who is doing what to whom?

Who gains?

Who loses?

Who pays?

Who gets protected?

Who gets punished?

Take corruption.

The word gets thrown around so constantly that it can almost become atmospheric.

But the basic idea is simple.

Corruption is public power redirected toward private benefit.

A job belonging to everybody is being used for somebody.

Do not only tell people there is a conflict of interest.

Show them the transaction.

What belonged to the public?

Who gets it instead?

What do the rest of us pay for?

This matters because Trump is very good at directing anger toward people with less power.

The immigrant.

The protester.

The person on welfare.

The professor.

The trans kid.

The journalist.

The bureaucrat nobody had heard of until that morning.

The emotional energy travels downward and sideways.

Truth-tellers should develop the opposite instinct.

Follow the anger upward.

Follow the money upward.

Follow the consequences downward.

If Americans are told that some vulnerable group is responsible for their economic pain, ask who actually sets the prices, owns the company, writes the tax rules, controls the workplace, receives the subsidy, or profits from the arrangement.

Sometimes reality will complicate the story Democrats would prefer to tell too.

Good.

That is what truth is supposed to do.

The point is not to replace one convenient villain with another.

It is to show people where power actually sits.

Politics begins as laws, appointments, budgets, regulations, court decisions, executive orders.

Eventually, it reaches a body.

Someone breathes the air.

Someone takes the medicine.

Someone misses the rent.

Someone works the shift.

Someone gets detained.

Someone loses a benefit.

Someone gets protected.

Someone does not.

Power becomes real when it reaches the body.

⸻

Outrage Is Not Leverage

And once you can see the power, you have to ask the question political outrage constantly avoids:

Where can it actually be moved?

Because outrage is not the same thing as leverage.

Something happens.

People post.

Politicians condemn it.

There is a rally.

Someone gives a very good speech.

A hashtag trends.

And then the person with the power to make the decision keeps making the decision.

People notice this.

Eventually “fight” begins to feel theatrical, not because the anger was fake, but because the anger never found the place where it could become power.

Every political struggle has some point where the outcome can still be altered.

A senator needs votes.

A judge can issue an injunction.

A governor can sign or veto a bill.

A company needs workers.

A politician needs reelection.

A policy needs people willing to implement it.

Sometimes the leverage is diffuse.

Sometimes it is weak.

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not currently have enough power to stop the thing.

But the question remains:

Who can change this decision, and what changes their calculation?

That is different from asking how angry everyone should be.

Understanding matters. Moral clarity matters. Public opinion matters.

But power is eventually a relationship.

Somebody wants something.

Somebody controls something.

Somebody needs something.

Somebody can deny it.

Find that relationship.

This is where “raise awareness” becomes insufficient.

Awareness of what?

For whom?

To produce what?

Directed at whom?

Before which decision?

Awareness should begin a chain.

If it ends at awareness, you have created a very informed audience.

You have not necessarily created power.

Even enormous protests illustrate the difference.

A movement like No Kings can give millions of people shared language. It can show isolated people that they are not alone. It can create relationships, confidence, and public legitimacy.

But expression still needs a destination.

Where does the energy go next?

Which vote changes?

Which institution becomes more willing to resist?

Which organization gets stronger?

A million people standing in the street do not automatically become a million units of leverage.

That does not diminish protest.

It finishes the sentence.

Trump understands dependency very well from the opposite direction.

A politician needs voters.

An official wants to keep his job.

A company wants favorable treatment.

An institution wants funding.

An ally wants protection.

He finds the dependency and pushes on it, often abusively and often in ways a democratic movement should not imitate.

But the underlying fact is still true.

Power works through dependency.

The democratic version is leverage.

Votes are leverage.

Money is leverage.

Labor is leverage.

Law is leverage.

Institutional authority is leverage.

Public legitimacy can become leverage.

Information can become leverage.

But everything visible is not leverage merely because a million people saw it.

A viral post seen by two million people may matter less to a close Senate vote than two hundred calls from the senator’s own constituents.

Not very cinematic.

Still power.

This also means Democrats have to become more honest about what they can and cannot do.

Sometimes they do not have the votes.

Sometimes the courts will not intervene.

Sometimes the president legally has the authority he is using.

Sometimes Republicans control the institution.

The answer is not to pretend sufficient courage magically eliminates those facts.

Say where the limits are.

“We cannot stop this vote today” is not the same thing as “There is nothing we can do.”

Maybe you can expose it.

Delay it.

Challenge its implementation.

Protect people from it.

Force everyone supporting it to attach their name to it.

Build the majority necessary to reverse it.

People can tolerate losing a battle more easily than they can tolerate feeling that nobody knows where the battle is.

⸻

Make the Fight Visible

And sometimes the real problem is not even a lack of action.

It is that the action is invisible.

This is where the public often stops believing Democrats.

They hear words suggesting an emergency and then see behavior suggesting a scheduling conflict.

“This is an unprecedented assault on democracy.”

Okay.

What are you doing?

“This is cruel and unlawful.”

Okay.

Who are you protecting?

“This is corruption.”

Okay.

What consequence are you seeking?

If politicians repeatedly tell the public that something is historically dangerous and the only thing the public sees afterward is another statement explaining how historically dangerous it is, eventually people stop believing either the danger or the politician.

But some of the resistance is much more real than that.

State attorneys general have brought case after case against Trump administration actions, sometimes blocking policies, restoring funding, protecting access to services, or forcing the administration back inside legal limits.

Those are verbs.

Filed.

Challenged.

Blocked.

Protected.

Restored.

Yet these victories often enter public consciousness as isolated legal-news items.

Another injunction.

Another case.

Another attorney general whose name somebody vaguely recognizes.

Then the news cycle resets around the next Trump declaration.

The action happened.

The story of the action never accumulated.

That is a mistake.

People need evidence that resistance can work.

Not always.

Not magically.

But here.

This action was attempted.

These people resisted.

This institution mattered.

This was protected.

This part failed.

This person blocked it.

This power remains.

Here is the next move.

That is agency made visible.

And truthful agency does not require pretending to win.

If you lost, say you lost.

If you did not have the votes, tell people which votes were missing.

If the court ruled against you, explain what that means.

If your strategy failed, admit it.

Trump constantly declares victory because victory itself is part of the reality he is attempting to create.

The opposition should not copy that.

Truthful agency is not omnipotence. It is orientation.

This is also where conviction stops being rhetorical and becomes behavioral.

Everybody is courageous at the press conference.

What happens when the belief costs a donor? A relationship? Poll numbers? Access? Political capital?

Not every compromise is cowardice. Politics without compromise can become another form of narcissism.

But a politician who describes the stakes as enormous and never appears willing to spend anything on them creates a credibility problem.

At some point the public asks:

If you believe this is what you say it is, why does your behavior look like this?

Your language should say it matters.

Your budget should say it matters.

Your vote should say it matters.

Your strategy should say it matters.

Your willingness to continue after the cameras leave should say it matters.

That is conviction with a body.

And if you are asking citizens to participate, give them a real mechanism.

“Make your voice heard.”

Where?

“Stand up for democracy.”

How?

“Fight back.”

Against which decision?

Call this office because this person has this vote.

Show up here because this board decides Tuesday.

Support this organization because it is bringing this case.

Volunteer in this district because this seat can change control of the chamber.

A real role has a mechanism.

Trump gives his supporters an extremely strong psychological role.

They are the patriots. The forgotten people. The fighters. The people taking their country back.

The story may be dishonest.

The human hunger underneath it is not.

People want to matter.

The democratic answer cannot be another cult around another leader.

It should be to give people a truer role.

Citizen.

Not follower.

Participant rather than spectator.

Someone whose vote, labor, money, attention, relationships, knowledge, courage, and participation can be organized with other people into power.

Democracy does not ask you to surrender yourself to a leader. It asks you to become harder to rule without your consent.

And people need victories.

Real ones.

Protect the program.

Win the injunction.

Defeat the appointment.

Save the seat.

Release the record.

Force the vote.

Reverse the cut.

When something works, tell people what they did.

Explain why it worked.

Let the victory become evidence.

Agency has to be experienced before it can be believed.

⸻

The Goal Is Not a Democratic Trump

There is one enormous danger in everything I have just said.

This could become another essay about messaging.

Democrats need better words. Better stories. More conviction. Funnier social media.

And then somehow America is saved.

No.

If your policy makes people’s lives worse, there is no sentence good enough to make that a communication problem.

If the candidate is terrible, the organization is weak, nobody trusts you, and you cannot govern competently, storytelling will not rescue you forever.

Politics is not advertising.

Material reality gets a vote too.

People notice whether their lives get easier or harder.

Whether the hospital stays open.

Whether the rent goes up.

Whether their government protects them.

A movement that learns to communicate beautifully while governing badly has not solved the problem.

It has become better at selling the problem.

So this is not a complete theory of how Democrats win.

Candidates matter. Policy matters. Competence matters. Money matters. Organization matters. Economic conditions matter. Luck matters.

What I am describing is narrower.

It is one reason a political opposition can possess real evidence, real moral objections, real public support, and sometimes real power and still look strangely helpless.

It has surrendered forms of agency it never needed to surrender.

And Democrats are not automatically the truth-tellers because Republicans lie.

That would be an extremely convenient conclusion for Democrats.

It would also be bullshit.

Democrats spin.

They hide failures.

They exaggerate.

They protect people who should probably not be protected.

They discover nuance very quickly when the uncomfortable fact belongs to their own side.

None of this works unless truth remains an actual discipline.

Not a brand.

Not the name you give your own propaganda because your intentions are nicer.

A truthful political story has to remain vulnerable to reality contradicting it.

Sometimes that means saying:

We were wrong.

This policy failed.

Our explanation was incomplete.

The person on the other side has a point.

This fact is inconvenient for us and it is still a fact.

That is the price of claiming reality as an ally.

Reality does not belong to Democrats either.

This is also why the answer cannot simply be to become more ruthless.

Trump looks strong because he refuses limits, so maybe Democrats should refuse more limits too.

No.

There is a difference between force and recklessness.

Not every confrontation creates leverage.

Not every compromise is cowardice.

Not every maximal demand is courage.

Sometimes it is easier to perform purity than to build enough power to accomplish something complicated.

The test is not whether an action looks like fighting.

The test is whether it protects people, changes incentives, builds capacity, creates legitimate consequences, or improves the chance of winning.

The goal is not a Democratic Trump.

That would be an extraordinarily depressing conclusion to the Trump era.

The answer to one man attempting to become the author of reality cannot simply be another man demanding the pen.

It has to be something more democratic than that.

A public capable of authorship too.

Trump offers a simple relationship with power.

Give it to me.

Trust me.

I will fight them.

I alone can fix it.

Your role is loyalty.

There is something psychologically attractive about that arrangement.

It reduces complexity.

It removes responsibility.

History gets a protagonist.

You get a seat in the audience and, if you are lucky, a red hat.

Democracy asks something harder.

It asks citizens to remain citizens.

To judge.

To organize.

To tolerate uncertainty.

To recognize when their own side is lying.

To lose elections without deciding the election was fake.

To win without treating victory as ownership.

To remain free without demanding freedom from responsibility.

That is less intoxicating than the strongman story.

It also contains a different kind of power.

Trump has built much of his political presence around the impression that he is the person making things happen.

He announces.

He attacks.

He declares.

He threatens.

He tells everyone who won.

And because everyone else responds, his centrality is constantly confirmed.

This is why Democrats cannot defeat him by spending the rest of his political life explaining him.

At some point they have to become more interested in what they are creating than in what he is destroying.

They need a story about the country that is not simply:

Trump must be stopped.

A country where ordinary people have enough material security that desperation is harder to weaponize.

Where laws constrain powerful people and do not merely punish weak ones.

Where public institutions belong to the public.

Where people can disagree without becoming enemies.

Where government can still do visible, competent things that improve human life.

Where freedom is not merely the privilege of whoever has enough money to avoid consequences.

Whatever that future is, it has to be real enough to build and specific enough to recognize.

Otherwise Trump remains the only person offering a future while everyone else offers resistance to his.

And resistance alone eventually becomes another form of dependence.

So I keep coming back to the same mistake.

Trump’s opponents have confused being constrained by truth with being condemned to reaction.

They are not the same thing.

Truth does not require passivity.

It requires discipline.

It limits what you can invent.

Good.

Keep the limit.

But everything else is still available.

Tell the truer story before the lie becomes the only story people know.

Say what you know with enough conviction that people can feel there is an actual person behind the words.

Say what you do not know too.

Show people where power enters their money, their bodies, their jobs, their rights, their families.

When something is wrong, find the person or institution capable of changing it.

Find what they depend on.

Build leverage.

Use it.

Then tell people what happened.

If you won, let them experience the victory.

If you lost, show them why.

If the next battle requires more power than you possess, tell them how that power gets built.

Give people something more useful than alarm.

Give them orientation.

Give them a role.

Give them evidence that their action can still change what happens.

Trump’s great political trick has been to live as though reality is a story he is writing.

His opponents should learn something from that without learning the lie.

Reality is not whatever we say it is.

But the future is not merely something that happens to us either.

People persuade.

They organize.

They resist.

They build institutions.

They change laws.

They protect one another.

They vote.

They refuse.

They cooperate.

They make new things possible and old things impossible.

That is authorship too.

Not the fantasy of one man announcing reality until everyone else submits to it.

The more difficult kind.
