What the Reflecting Pool Reflected

A rushed patriotic spectacle began to peel. The Trump administration called it sabotage—and turned an American Olympian into the culprit.

David Hearn represented the United States at three Olympic Games before the United States accused him of attacking itself.

In June, the former champion canoeist was arrested after reaching into the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool and pulling at part of its newly installed blue surface. Federal prosecutors later indicted him for felony destruction of government property.

Jeanine Pirro, Trump’s handpicked U.S. attorney for Washington, described it as a deliberate attack on one of the nation’s most important monuments. The White House called the damage sabotage committed by people driven by “hatred for America.”

Interior Secretary Doug Burgum went on television and declared himself certain. Asked whether he was 100 percent sure vandalism had caused the damage, he answered, “Oh, yes absolutely.”

The material could not simply peel away, he said. There was no possible way.

But it already had.

Government records later showed that the pool’s new surface was blistering, separating and peeling before Hearn touched it. Engineers had identified weak areas around the perimeter. Overspray and poor bonding had left portions vulnerable to being pulled loose. When investigators finally examined the drained pool and reviewed the construction records, they found defects extending far beyond the place where Hearn had stood.

The government had evidence that the pool was failing before Hearn arrived.

But it had an enemy before it had an explanation.

The project was part of Washington’s transformation for America’s 250th birthday. The Reflecting Pool would receive a new blue surface. It would look cleaner, grander and more patriotic in time for the celebration.

The resurfacing was awarded through a no-bid contract whose value reportedly grew to roughly $14.7 million. The work proceeded under intense deadline pressure.

Trump made the project personally his. He spoke of consulting his pool contractor and presented the choice as another demonstration of his business genius: a superior product, delivered faster, for less money, built to last forty or fifty years.

Before the work had proved itself, Trump had already turned it into proof of himself.

This has always been one of his central promises. He knows the best people. He recognizes what experts miss. Where institutions hesitate, he decides. Where professionals complicate, he cuts through.

The pool would succeed because he had chosen it.

Burgum was “fantastic.” The contractor had done a “fantastic job.” When Trump appointed Pirro, he called her “incredibly well qualified” and “in a class by herself.”

Everyone had been declared excellent before reality completed its inspection.

Then the blue surface began to peel.

The administration faced two possible questions.

What went wrong with the work?

Or:

Who did this to us?

It chose the second.

Failure became sabotage. People touching the loose material became radicals and enemies. A construction problem became evidence that people who hated America were attacking its monuments.

The administration did not merely choose the wrong explanation. It gave the explanation a villain.

Once the peeling had been defined as an attack on the country, uncertainty itself became suspicious. To ask whether the installation had failed was to sound as though you were defending the people supposedly trying to destroy the Lincoln Memorial.

Certainty replaced inspection.

Then certainty acquired the power of law.

Hearn appears to have pulled at material near the pool’s edge. Witnesses said he continued after being told to stop. His defense maintained that he was examining material that had already separated.

Whatever one thinks of his conduct, the coating was failing before he encountered it. The government’s fuller records later made it impossible to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that he had caused the damage attributed to him.

But by then, Hearn had already been indicted on a federal felony charge.

The official story did not remain a boast, a television performance or a White House press release. It descended through the government until it became another person’s criminal jeopardy.

Hearn’s Olympic history does not make him more deserving of justice than anyone else. Every citizen deserves restraint from a state powerful enough to imprison him.

But his history makes the contradiction impossible to miss.

Hearn represented the United States in canoe slalom at the 1992, 1996 and 2000 Olympic Games. He once carried the country’s name before the world.

Now the country carried his name as that of a vandal.

The administration claimed to be defending America’s history, dignity and national symbols. Yet to preserve the appearance that its patriotic project had been competently executed, it endangered an American who had spent part of his life representing the nation.

It protected the image of America at the expense of an American.

Some forms of patriotism love surfaces: flags, monuments, ceremonies, colors, slogans and enemies against whom the nation can define itself.

A deeper patriotism accepts responsibility for what the nation does to its people.

The Reflecting Pool was prepared for the first kind. Hearn encountered the absence of the second.

Eventually, someone looked.

After visiting the drained pool and obtaining fuller records from the Interior Department, Pirro’s office changed course. Prosecutors acknowledged widespread installation problems and moved to dismiss Hearn’s case without prejudice.

The motion did not formally declare that Hearn had never done anything wrong. Nor did it establish that nobody had ever interfered with the surface.

It established something narrower and more damaging to the government’s story: the pool had been failing before Hearn touched it, the evidence could not support the charge beyond a reasonable doubt, and prosecutors would not have pursued the case in the same way had they possessed the fuller information earlier.

Pirro should not be remade into the hero. Her office brought the prosecution and publicly promoted its case.

But confronted with contradictory evidence, it corrected itself.

Trump responded by saying he disagreed with Pirro “100%.”

The woman he had called “in a class by herself” had exercised the judgment for which she was supposedly chosen. She examined the evidence and reached a conclusion he did not want.

She had not suddenly become less qualified.

She had become less reflective of Trump.

That is what the promise of “the best people” can mean inside a loyalty system. Their value does not lie in the independence of their judgment. It lies in their ability to return the leader’s preferred image.

They are mirrors on probation.

Burgum reflected Trump’s certainty and was praised. The contractor’s work reflected Trump’s image of himself as a master builder and was called fantastic. Pirro’s authority was valuable while it supported the vandalism story.

When her office reflected something else, her judgment became another thing to reject.

The system first blamed the citizen who touched the peeling surface.

Then it rejected the official who acknowledged why it was peeling.

This is more revealing than hypocrisy or incompetence. It shows what happens when a leader claims a public success as evidence of himself and reality begins to damage the evidence.

The failure must go somewhere.

A functioning institution can say: We rushed. We were wrong. We accused too quickly. We must repair the physical damage and the damage done to the person we blamed.

Power that cannot absorb that knowledge must find someone else to carry it.

A vandal.

A radical.

A disloyal official.

Someone who touched the loose edge.

This is one of the deepest mechanisms of authoritarian politics. The leader’s image becomes too important to be revised by ordinary reality. Evidence that confirms him is truth. Evidence that contradicts him becomes incompetence, betrayal or attack.

The leader’s humiliation becomes someone else’s emergency.

America was preparing to celebrate its 250th birthday. Its government laid a bright blue surface across the nation’s most famous mirror. The work was rushed. The surface peeled. Officials called the failure sabotage and blamed an American Olympian who touched what had already come loose.

When the evidence finally pointed back toward the people who had ordered, praised and defended the work, the president rejected the reflection.

The Reflecting Pool did not fail to perform its function.

It reflected exactly what stood before it.
