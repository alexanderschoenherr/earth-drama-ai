The Audit

What could Trump do that would actually count against him?

There is something almost too perfect about the fact that this story is about an audit.

Donald Trump was wronged by the IRS.

An IRS contractor stole his confidential tax information and leaked it to the press. He pleaded guilty and went to prison.

It happened.

Then Trump came back to power.

His former personal lawyer, Todd Blanche, became acting attorney general.

And Blanche issued an order purporting to bar the IRS from conducting future tax audits of Trump, his sons, and their entities.

Trump was wronged by the IRS.

Then his government tried to make him unauditable by the IRS.

The answer to a bad audit became no audit.

And there is an even stranger part.

Trump was suing the government while he was president of that government.

The citizen bringing the claim and the man controlling the state defending against it were the same man. His former personal lawyer was now acting on behalf of the United States.

A republic is supposed to keep these things separate.

The ruler is not the republic.

His interests are not automatically its interests.

His grievances are not automatically its grievances.

Judge Kathleen Williams later found that Trump had used the lawsuit for an improper purpose and wrote that Blanche’s provision purporting to bar future audits “directly contravenes” federal law.

And two Republican senators, John Cornyn and Thom Tillis, are resisting Blanche’s confirmation while demanding assurances around the arrangement.

That matters.

Because the point is not that Republicans need to become Democrats.

The point is that Republicans need to remain capable of telling a Republican president no.

Anyone can demand an audit of the enemy.

The republican test is whether you permit the audit of your own.

Trump really was wronged.

And being wronged does not make everything you do afterward right.

Protection from abuse is not exemption from accountability.

Trump has built an extraordinary amount of political power in the space between those two sentences.

His strongest stories are often not invented from nothing.

There is usually something there.

A mistake.

An injustice.

An abuse.

A wound.

Then the fact becomes a grievance.

The grievance becomes a story.

The story becomes a worldview.

The worldview becomes a warrant.

There really were serious failures in the FBI’s surveillance of former Trump campaign adviser Carter Page.

That does not make the entire Russia investigation a fabricated conspiracy.

An FBI document used during the Mar-a-Lago search really did contain language about deadly force.

That did not mean Joe Biden sent the FBI to assassinate Trump.

Trump does not always need the whole story to be true.

He needs one part of it to be undeniable.

Challenge the larger claim and the argument retreats into the part that is true.

So you’re saying the IRS leak never happened?

No.

So you’re saying the FBI did nothing wrong?

No.

Something happened.

The question is what it proves.

The most powerful falsehoods contain truth.

Truth gives them somewhere to hide.

Trump does not always invent the wound.

He enlarges its jurisdiction.

Until the wound gets to decide who can be trusted, which institutions are legitimate, which rules still apply, what the wounded man is now entitled to do.

The grievance becomes a warrant.

The warrant becomes power.

And power eventually asks for exemption.

Which is why the audit is such a perfect image.

An audit is something outside you checking whether your story about yourself is true.

The IRS audits money.

Institutions audit power.

Evidence audits belief.

Reality audits us all.

An audit says:

You do not get to grade yourself.

Nobody gets to be their own auditor.

That is republican government in miniature.

Power has to answer to something outside itself.

Trump’s language of “weaponization” is powerful because it begins with something true.

Government can be weaponized.

The IRS can abuse power. The FBI can abuse power. Prosecutors can abuse power. Presidents can abuse power.

But the existence of bad auditors is an argument for better audits.

Not for an unauditable president.

There is a difference between disarming a weapon and taking possession of it.

If “deweaponization” means:

Stop scrutinizing us.

Start punishing them.

Nothing has been deweaponized.

The weapon has changed hands.

And there is an even more dangerous move:

Turn accountability itself into persecution.

Then everything reverses.

The investigation proves the investigators are corrupt.

The court ruling proves the court is corrupt.

The election loss proves the election is corrupt.

The Republican who objects proves he is a traitor.

The institution that resists proves it was part of the conspiracy all along.

Every check on power becomes evidence that power needs fewer checks.

It is a remarkable machine.

The institution investigates the leader. That proves the institution is weaponized. The leader attacks the institution. The institution resists. That proves it is weaponized.

So the leader needs more power.

And when that power is resisted?

More proof.

There is no natural stopping point inside that logic.

Trump has another powerful ingredient: the wound.

He is the billionaire and the victim.

The president and the persecuted outsider.

The man running the government and the man supposedly hunted by it.

Power normally invites scrutiny.

Victimhood can make scrutiny look cruel.

Power starts to look like self-defense.

Restraint starts to look like another attack.

Your wound deserves recognition.

It does not deserve sovereignty.

A victim can become a perpetrator.

A movement can be mistreated and still become corrupt.

A president can be wronged and still abuse power.

Accountability is the price power pays for legitimacy.

Royal power wants legitimacy without the price.

So here is the question I wish every Trump supporter would answer.

Not even:

What could Trump do that would make you stop supporting him?

Start smaller.

What could Trump do that would count against him?

What evidence could actually lower your judgment of him?

Is there anything?

You can think Trump has done terrible things and still vote for him.

You may care more about immigration, judges, taxes, foreign policy, abortion, culture, or the alternative candidate. You can believe this IRS arrangement is corrupt and still conclude that, on balance, you support him.

That is judgment.

The question is whether the judgment can still move.

Because there is another condition.

Every investigation is corrupt before you know what it finds.

Every court illegitimate when it rules against him.

Every election suspicious when he loses.

Every Republican disloyal when he objects.

Every institution capable of returning a negative verdict disqualified before the verdict arrives.

Then something has reversed.

At first:

I support Trump because I judge him to be right.

Eventually:

I judge things to be right because Trump supports them.

Loyalty stops following judgment.

It starts replacing it.

The problem is not loyalty.

The question is whether loyalty remains subordinate to judgment.

If nothing is allowed to count against the thing you are loyal to, it is no longer being judged.

And if no evidence can count against a leader, support is no longer a conclusion.

It has become a premise.

Now we are talking about something older than Trump.

Royal versus republican.

Royal power seeks exemption.

Republican power submits to examination.

Royal power says:

Trust the ruler.

Republican power says:

Test the ruler.

And royal power does something else.

It collapses the ruler and the realm.

His interests become the nation’s interests.

His enemies become the nation’s enemies.

His grievances become public grievances.

His protection becomes public necessity.

A republic insists otherwise.

The ruler is not the republic.

The royal fantasy is not merely to win the case.

It is to abolish the court.

Not:

Judge me innocent.

But:

Who are you to judge me?

The king becomes his own court.

His own jury.

His own appeal.

Eventually, his own reality.

The king’s oldest temptation is to become unauditable.

America was founded in revolt against precisely this.

No king.

No sovereign man above the law.

But America has never contained only one America.

Alongside the republic has always lived another dream.

The exceptional man.

The winner.

The billionaire.

The celebrity.

The conqueror.

The man too great for ordinary restraints.

One America says:

No one is above the law.

Another whispers:

Unless he is great enough.

Trump is not simply America’s opposite.

He is one of America’s possibilities.

He did not betray some pure America that existed before him.

He activated one of the Americas that was already here.

A country founded in rebellion against kings became fascinated by a man who behaves like one.

And it would be comfortable to stop there.

Trump.

MAGA.

Them.

But everyone has something they do not want audited.

A hero.

A grievance.

A version of themselves.

We want truth.

But often only the kind that leaves us intact.

That is why being corrected can feel so much like being defeated.

To say “I was wrong” can feel like surrender. Like humiliation. Like giving the enemy something.

But being corrected is not the same as being conquered.

A mind that cannot survive correction eventually has no choice but to protect itself from reality.

Someone really can betray you.

An institution really can fail you.

A country really can wrong you.

And from inside the injury, it becomes terribly easy to believe the wound has made you right about everything that follows.

It has not.

A wound can explain you.

It cannot absolve you.

To remain auditable is to accept something deeply uncomfortable:

I do not have final jurisdiction over reality.

Neither does a president.

Neither does a political movement.

Neither does a nation.

Reality is the one auditor we cannot fire.

A free society depends on remaining correctable.

A healthy republic must be able to say:

We were wrong.

A healthy movement:

Our leader was wrong.

A healthy person:

I was wrong.

Anything that loses that capacity stops learning.

Anything that cannot learn eventually leaves reality behind.

The deepest danger is not that Donald Trump wants to escape an audit.

Power has always wanted that.

The deeper danger is that millions of people might come to believe that auditing their leader is itself an injustice.

Then the auditor becomes the enemy.

Correction becomes persecution.

Restraint becomes oppression.

Loyalty replaces judgment.

Republican power becomes royal.

Trump is still auditable.

The question is whether we are.

What could count against what you believe?

What wound have you turned into a warrant?

What have you protected from reality?

What have you made unauditable?

Everyone wants the truth.

Until the audit comes.
