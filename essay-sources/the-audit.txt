The Audit

What could Trump do that would actually count against him?

Donald Trump sued his own government.

Then Todd Blanche—Trump’s former personal lawyer, now the acting attorney general of the United States—signed a one-page order attempting to carry out the most extraordinary part of the settlement.

The order bore the seal of the Department of Justice and a single signature: Blanche’s.

It purported to release Trump, his sons, their families, their trusts, their companies, their affiliates, and their subsidiaries from a sweeping range of federal claims, examinations, investigations, and related reviews.

The protections were not limitless. They applied to matters that existed or could have been asserted as of May 18, 2026, including tax returns filed before that date.

But within those boundaries, the language was enormous.

The United States “RELEASES, WAIVES, ACQUITS, and FOREVER DISCHARGES” the plaintiffs, the order declared, and is “FOREVER BARRED and PRECLUDED” from pursuing them.

One page. One signature.

An entire family business empire beneath a shield.

Trump had sued the IRS and the Treasury Department—agencies inside the executive branch he controlled. His former lawyer, now speaking for the government, signed the order intended to protect him.

A federal judge later prevented the settlement terms from taking legal effect.

Judge Kathleen Williams concluded that the lawsuit had been filed for an improper purpose and that there had never really been two opposing sides. The supposed adversaries shared what she called a “unitary interest.” The court, she wrote, had been used to lend legitimacy to a predetermined arrangement benefiting Trump and people affiliated with him.

Trump controlled the government he was suing.

Blanche represented that government.

And Blanche signed the order protecting Trump.

Trump stood on both sides of the courtroom.

Even Republican senators objected. They held up Blanche’s confirmation while demanding assurances about both the tax protections and a $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund” created by the broader settlement.

That matters.

This was not simply another charge originating with Democrats or the press. Members of Trump’s own party looked at the arrangement and asked where the protection ended.

This is the kind of story that makes ordinary political language feel inadequate.

“Conflict of interest” sounds too mild. “Hypocrisy” sounds too familiar. Even “corruption” can feel strangely routine beside the image itself: the billionaire populist, champion of the forgotten taxpayer, using the machinery of the state to protect himself, his family, and his companies from the tax collector.

How could anyone still believe this man is fighting for ordinary people?

How could someone who despises elite immunity, government favoritism, and two systems of justice look at this and continue to see Trump as the tribune of the common man?

That is the obvious question.

It is not yet the deepest one.

Because Trump did not invent the original wrong.

An IRS contractor named Charles Littlejohn really did steal Trump’s private tax information and disclose it to the press. He also stole the records of thousands of other taxpayers. He pleaded guilty and was sentenced to five years in prison.

This was not imaginary persecution. It was a crime, a violation of public trust, and an abuse of access to government power.

Something genuinely wrong happened to Trump.

That does not weaken the case against what followed.

It clarifies it.

A person can be genuinely mistreated and still seek an unjust remedy. Those realities are not mutually exclusive. Quite often, they live inside the same story.

There is a temptation—political, moral, and deeply human—to believe that once someone has truly been wronged, everything they do in response inherits the righteousness of the original wound.

But it doesn’t.

Being betrayed does not make every suspicion true. Being attacked does not make every retaliation just. Being persecuted does not make every future accusation persecution.

And being violated by an institution does not entitle you to permanent exemption from that institution’s lawful scrutiny.

Protection from abuse is not exemption from accountability.

The proper response to the theft of Trump’s tax information was straightforward: find the offender, prosecute him, punish him, repair the institutional failure, compensate legitimate harm, and strengthen protections for every taxpayer.

Much of that happened. Littlejohn was caught, convicted, and imprisoned.

Justice did not ignore the injury.

But sometimes a wound begins to demand more than justice.

The logic goes something like this: the institution failed once, so it may never judge me again. One official abused his power, so every future exercise of power against me must be corrupt. Someone once lied about me, so every criticism from now on must be another lie.

I was treated unfairly, so fairness itself must now bend around me.

The grievance stops being one claim among others—something that can be examined, weighed, and placed beside other facts.

It becomes the standard by which everything else is judged.

This is one of Trump’s greatest political gifts.

He does not always invent his stories from nothing. Often, there is something real inside them: an institutional failure, a serious error, a genuine abuse, a phrase pulled from an actual document, a wound that really happened.

That fragment of truth becomes the foundation for a much larger structure—one the original fact could never support on its own.

And when someone challenges the structure, Trump points back to the foundation.

Are you saying the leak never happened?

Are you saying the FBI never made serious mistakes?

Are you saying there was not a single election irregularity anywhere?

Are you saying the document did not contain the words “deadly force”?

No.

Something happened.

That is precisely why the larger story works.

The most powerful falsehoods contain truth.

They need one fact strong enough to carry conclusions the fact itself does not prove.

Trump does not need the entire story to be true. He needs one part to be true enough to protect the rest.

The real injury becomes evidence of the imagined conspiracy. The isolated failure becomes proof of total corruption. The specific wrongdoing becomes permission for retaliation, immunity, loyalty, and fewer restraints.

The grievance becomes a warrant.

Trump takes a real wound and crowns it king.

You can see the pattern in his response to the FBI search of Mar-a-Lago.

The FBI’s operational plan contained language authorizing the use of “deadly force.”

That was true. The words were there.

Trump took those words and told the country that Joe Biden’s government had been prepared to kill him.

But the language was standard Justice Department policy restricting the use of force to situations involving an immediate threat. Trump was not there, and the search had been scheduled while he was away in part to avoid a confrontation.

That explanation could not compete with the phrase itself:

Deadly force.

A real phrase became proof of an attempted execution. A bureaucratic provision became an assassination plot.

Trump did not have to fabricate the document. He only had to enlarge its meaning until the document appeared to say something it didn’t.

The words were there.

The conclusion was not.

The same pattern appeared in the investigation of Trump’s relationship with Russia.

The FBI made serious errors. Applications to surveil former Trump campaign adviser Carter Page contained significant inaccuracies and omissions. Information that weakened the case was not placed before the court, making the evidence appear stronger than it was.

Those failures mattered. They deserved investigation. They damaged trust in institutions with extraordinary power, and they gave Trump a legitimate grievance.

But they did not prove that every concern surrounding Russia was invented or that every investigator, official, journalist, witness, and judge belonged to one coordinated conspiracy.

An institution can behave badly without everything it has examined becoming false.

A dishonest auditor does not make the subject of the audit permanently unauditable.

That distinction is easy to state and difficult to hold.

People want the world to sort itself cleanly. Either the institution is trustworthy or corrupt. Either the accused is guilty or persecuted. Either the investigation was legitimate or everything connected to it was a fraud.

Reality is rarely that obedient.

An institution can mistreat someone and still uncover something true about him. A leader can be persecuted in one instance and corrupt in another. An investigator can abuse his power without every person he investigates becoming innocent.

But Trump’s story rarely stops at: the FBI committed serious misconduct.

It becomes: the FBI is illegitimate whenever it investigates me.

The wrongdoing of particular officials spreads until it contaminates the whole institution. Then it moves outward.

The judge is corrupt. The prosecutor is corrupt. The reporter is corrupt. The Republican who objects is corrupt. The election official who refuses to change the result is corrupt.

The institution no longer needs to do anything wrong.

Its disagreement becomes proof of its corruption.

This is where grievance becomes more dangerous than exaggeration. It begins destroying the possibility of correction.

Trump does not merely deny an accusation. He attacks the legitimacy of being accused. He does not merely dispute a verdict. He disqualifies the judge. He does not merely argue that an investigation is wrong. He turns the investigation itself into evidence that the investigators are his enemies.

The question stops being:

Did Trump do what he is accused of doing?

It becomes:

Why are these corrupt people attacking Trump again?

The accusation disappears beneath the story of persecution. Because persecution is already assumed, every new accusation arrives pre-invalidated.

The facts no longer determine whether persecution occurred.

The belief in persecution determines what the facts are allowed to mean.

This may be the most dangerous transformation in the entire Trump phenomenon.

The deeper move is not merely denying accountability.

It is redefining accountability itself as persecution.

Once that happens, every check on power becomes evidence that power needs fewer checks.

The investigation proves the investigators must be controlled. The ruling proves the court must be attacked. The criticism proves the press must be discredited. The election loss proves the election system cannot be trusted. The Republican who resists proves the party must be purified.

The audit proves the auditor is an enemy.

The logic has no natural stopping point.

An institution investigates Trump. The investigation becomes proof that the institution is weaponized. Trump attacks the institution. The institution resists. Its resistance becomes further proof of weaponization. Trump then demands more power to defeat it.

Soon every independent center of judgment becomes an obstacle to justice as defined by Trump.

Justice gradually stops meaning the impartial application of a standard.

It starts meaning victory for the leader.

It is worth slowing down here, because government institutions really can be weaponized.

The IRS has been abused. The FBI has been abused. Prosecutors have abused their discretion. Intelligence agencies have deceived the public. Presidents have turned the machinery of the state against their enemies.

Distrust does not come from nowhere.

The answer is not blind faith in institutions. But neither is it a government whose legitimacy depends on whether its decisions benefit one man.

The opposite of a weaponized institution is an impartial institution.

It is not an institution loyal to your side.

If the FBI investigates your enemies but never your allies, it has not been deweaponized. The weapon has simply changed hands.

If prosecutors are corrupt when they investigate Trump but righteous when they investigate Trump’s opponents, the principle was never opposition to political prosecution.

The principle was who gets prosecuted.

And if government scrutiny is tyranny when directed at the president but justice when directed by the president, the problem was never government power.

The problem was who controlled it.

That is the difference between reform and revenge.

Reform tries to make the rule fair for everyone.

Revenge tries to take possession of the rule.

Trump does not merely promise to stop government weaponization. His politics repeatedly turns toward removing, investigating, or punishing the people he says weaponized it against him—and replacing them with people who will not pose the same threat.

From inside the grievance, that can feel like justice.

From outside it, something else becomes visible.

The leader is not trying to end political power.

He is trying to become its rightful owner.

Return for a moment to the page.

The Department of Justice seal.

The single signature.

The former lawyer signing for the government in an arrangement benefiting the former client.

Trump was genuinely wronged. Then the man once hired to defend him became the man speaking for the United States. And the government he represented issued an order meant to shield the man he once represented.

The injury was real.

The proposed remedy brought the injured man closer to controlling the machinery that could judge him.

The grievance became a government.

That does not happen through force alone.

It requires a story that can make greater power feel like greater protection. It requires the most powerful man in the country to remain, somehow, its most endangered victim.

Trump has mastered that contradiction.

He is the billionaire and the forgotten man. The president and the outsider. The ruler and the rebel. The commander of the government and the target of the government.

The more power he holds, the more powerful the conspiracy against him must become. And the more powerful the conspiracy becomes, the more power he appears to need.

His victimhood is not the opposite of his power.

It is one of its sources.

Power alone invites suspicion. Victimhood gives power moral permission.

A powerful man asking to escape accountability looks corrupt. A persecuted man asking for the strength to defeat his persecutors looks heroic.

The action can be the same.

The story changes its meaning.

That is why Trump’s followers do not necessarily experience every expansion of his authority as submission to a ruler. Many experience it as self-defense.

They see the institutions coming for Trump as the same institutions that ignored, mocked, investigated, censored, displaced, or betrayed people like them.

Trump’s wounds merge with theirs. His enemies become their enemies. His survival becomes their vindication. His demand for power begins to feel like their demand to finally be protected.

This is not true of every person who votes for him. Political support can be reluctant, transactional, strategic, or based on a few issues that have little to do with personal devotion.

But for those who have fused their identity with his, the road to becoming unauditable is made of understandable steps.

An institution lies. Trust weakens. Another institution fails. Suspicion spreads. A leader names the failure. People feel seen. The leader is attacked. The attack confirms the diagnosis. He asks for more power.

The request feels less like domination than defense.

Then one day, every mechanism capable of correcting the leader has been absorbed into the story of why the leader must not be corrected.

The question is no longer merely whether the government can audit Donald Trump.

The deeper question is whether the people who believe in him still can.

That question has to be asked carefully.

A person can believe Trump did something wrong and still vote for him.

Politics is full of compromises. A voter may dislike his conduct but prefer his immigration policy. They may condemn his self-dealing but care more about taxes, judges, abortion, war, crime, the economy, or the Democratic alternative.

They may believe he is deeply flawed and still conclude that he is the better choice.

We can argue with that calculation. We can question what it reveals about their priorities.

But it is still a calculation.

The voter has examined Trump, found something wrong, weighed it against other concerns, and made a decision. The audit happened. Trump did not pass every part of it. The voter simply decided his failures were not disqualifying.

That is different from a belief system in which Trump cannot fail.

Different from a loyalty that permits no evidence to count against him.

So the central question is not only:

What could Trump do that would make you stop supporting him?

It is even more basic:

What could Trump do that you would be willing to call wrong?

Is there anything?

Could he use government power to enrich himself? Protect his companies from scrutiny ordinary Americans must endure? Investigate an enemy for personal revenge? Knowingly lie about an election he lost? Ask public officials to place loyalty to him above their duty to the law?

What would count?

Not what would cause you to vote for a Democrat. Not what would make you trust the media. Not what would force you to abandon every grievance you have ever had against the government.

What would simply allow you to say:

Trump was wrong.

That is the minimum requirement of political judgment.

A citizen has to be able to distinguish between the leader and the truth. Between what serves the movement and what serves the man. Between defending someone from injustice and defending him from the consequences of his own choices.

Without that distinction, loyalty swallows judgment.

The supporter no longer asks whether an accusation is true. They ask who made it.

A Democrat accused him. False.

A prosecutor charged him. Weaponized.

A judge ruled against him. Corrupt.

A Republican criticized him. RINO.

A former ally described what he witnessed. Disgruntled.

His own recorded words become misleading, taken out of context, sarcastic, joking, exaggerated, misunderstood, or secretly brilliant.

Any one of those defenses might occasionally be right. Investigations can be partisan. Witnesses can lie. Recordings can be stripped of context.

But when every road leads to innocence, innocence is no longer a finding.

It has become a rule.

The audit no longer tests the leader.

It exists to clear him.

This is how support stops being a conclusion and becomes a premise.

A conclusion can be revised by new information. A premise decides what new information is allowed to mean.

Someone who concludes that Trump is fighting corruption can still discover that Trump is corrupt.

Someone who begins with the premise that Trump is the only man capable of fighting corruption has to reinterpret his corruption as strategy, self-defense, persecution, exaggeration, or the lesser evil.

The belief protects itself.

That is why one more scandal does not necessarily break the spell.

We imagine political persuasion as a scale. Put enough evidence on one side, and eventually the balance must change.

But identity is not always a scale.

Sometimes it is a fortress.

Friendly facts are admitted. Hostile facts are searched, disarmed, renamed, and sent back outside.

Eventually, the attacks themselves become proof that whatever is inside must be precious.

Why would they fight him this hard if he were not dangerous to them?

Why would there be so many investigations if he were not threatening the system?

The accumulation of evidence reverses direction. Instead of weakening the belief, it confirms the size of the conspiracy required to explain it away.

Contradiction becomes confirmation.

This is where political judgment begins to resemble faith.

Not because every Trump supporter worships him. Not because politics and religion are identical. But because the belief can acquire the self-sealing structure of a system in which persecution confirms chosenness.

They hate him because he tells the truth. They attack him because he threatens their power. They charge him because he is innocent.

The more the world contradicts the story, the more heroic the story becomes.

Trump does not merely survive the accusation.

He feeds on it.

Every indictment can become another station in the passion. Every courtroom another stage. Every mug shot another icon. Every restraint another crown of thorns placed on the man who says he is suffering for his people.

Legal jeopardy becomes sacred drama. The accusation itself becomes evidence of sacrifice.

His supporters do not necessarily experience themselves as excusing corruption. They experience themselves as refusing to participate in a ritual humiliation organized by people they already distrust.

That is why shouting more evidence at them often fails.

The evidence is arriving from institutions the story has already taught them not to trust.

The auditor has been disqualified before the audit begins.

But a democracy cannot function if citizens outsource judgment to loyalty. It cannot survive if accountability is valid only when directed at the other side.

A political movement remains democratic only while its members can still say no to their own leader.

No, that is false.

No, that is corrupt.

No, that is too far.

No, your grievance does not justify this.

No, supporting you does not require surrendering my judgment to you.

That word—no—is one of the smallest institutions in a free society.

Before courts, elections, and constitutions can protect a republic, there has to be a citizen capable of withholding consent. A follower capable of becoming a critic. An ally capable of choosing the law over the leader. A believer capable of letting the beloved fail.

That is why the resistance of Republican senators matters, even if it is limited.

The deeper question is whether any boundary remains that loyalty cannot erase.

Whether the party still contains people capable of telling its leader:

You were wronged.

And this is still wrong.

That sentence recognizes the wound without kneeling before it.

Because the unauditable leader ultimately demands more than support.

He demands interpretive sovereignty: the right to decide in advance what every investigation means, which institutions are legitimate, and what reality is permitted to say about him.

The phrase may sound abstract. The practice is not.

The court speaks. Trump tells his followers what the ruling really means.

An investigator presents evidence. Trump tells them why the investigator cannot be trusted.

The voters deliver a result. Trump tells them whether the result counts.

The institution speaks, but the leader reserves the right to translate.

Once enough people grant him that right, formal immunity becomes almost secondary. He may still be investigated, charged, criticized, and audited.

But nothing outside him can produce a binding conclusion.

The institutions speak. The leader translates. The followers accept the translation.

The audit occurs, but the subject controls what the audit is allowed to mean.

At that point, the ruler is approaching an ancient form of power.

Not because he wears a crown. Not because the Constitution has disappeared. But because he is no longer merely one man operating inside the system.

He is becoming the measure by which the system itself is judged.

If the law serves him, the law is just. If it restrains him, the law is corrupt. If the institution protects him, it is legitimate. If it examines him, it has been weaponized. If the people choose him, the election is sacred. If they reject him, the election is stolen.

The leader no longer answers to the standard.

The standard begins answering to the leader.

That is where kingship begins.

Not with a throne. Not even with the end of elections.

Kingship begins when the ruler stops understanding himself as one person inside a system and begins seeing himself as the standard by which the system should be judged.

The ancient temptation of power is not merely to break the rules.

It is to become the source of the rules.

A president is supposed to be something very different: temporary, limited, conditional, and answerable to laws he did not write alone.

A president occupies the office.

A king absorbs it.

A president may be wronged by the state.

A king concludes that the state has no right to judge him again.

This is why the audit is such a perfect image.

An audit means that something outside you gets to examine your account of yourself.

You do not choose the evidence. You do not decide in advance what it means. You do not declare yourself cleared and call the process complete.

Someone else looks at the books and gets to say:

This does not add up.

That is not merely how taxation works.

It is how republican government works.

Congress audits the executive. Courts audit the law. Journalists audit official stories. Elections audit governments. Citizens audit all of them.

The democratic system is built around a humbling proposition:

You are not the final authority on your own use of power.

Nobody gets to be their own auditor.

Power must answer to something outside itself because power is extremely good at explaining itself to itself. Every abuse has a reason. Every retaliation has a justification. Every exemption has a history. Every ruler can describe the emergency that made restraint impossible.

That is why free societies build external limits instead of trusting rulers to judge themselves fairly.

The American rebellion against monarchy was, at heart, a rebellion against the idea that one human being could become politically sacred.

Thomas Paine wrote that in America, the law would be king.

The crown would not disappear. It would move from the head of a man to the authority of a principle.

That is the republican promise: the ruler stands beneath the law. The office is greater than the man because the man must eventually leave it.

But America has always carried another dream inside that dream.

The dream of the exceptional man.

The man too successful for ordinary limits. Too wealthy to be questioned. Too strong to apologize. The man whose victory proves his virtue, whose wealth proves his wisdom, whose domination proves his fitness to dominate.

America says no one is above the law.

But America also worships the person who appears powerful enough to rise above everything else.

The billionaire. The celebrity. The dealmaker. The winner.

We claim to distrust kings, but we adore kingly scale.

We reject inherited royalty and manufacture our own through wealth, fame, spectacle, and attention. We say every citizen is equal, then build entire industries around watching exceptional people live under different rules and different consequences.

Trump did not create this contradiction.

He discovered it inside us.

He understood that Americans could reject the language of monarchy while responding deeply to its emotional structure.

The palace became a penthouse. The crown became a brand. The royal court became television.

The king no longer needed divine blood. He needed ratings, wealth, and the power to dominate the national imagination so completely that every institution appeared smaller beside him.

Trump did not present himself as a humble public servant. He presented himself as the extraordinary man who could succeed precisely because he was not like ordinary politicians.

Richer. Stronger. Less restrained. Less ashamed.

The qualities that should have made greater scrutiny necessary became the argument for why scrutiny was an obstacle.

His norm-breaking became authenticity. His shamelessness became strength. His refusal to accept limits became proof that he could defeat the people imposing limits on everyone else.

People who felt powerless experienced his power as their own.

This is one of the oldest bargains between the public and the strongman:

Give me your judgment, and I will give you my force.

Let me stand above the rules, and I will use that height against your enemies.

The bargain feels democratic because the ruler is chosen. It feels populist because he speaks in the name of the people. It feels rebellious because he attacks institutions.

But the emotional structure is royal.

The leader does not merely represent the people. He begins to embody them.

An insult to him becomes an insult to them. A verdict against him becomes a verdict against them. His enemies become enemies of the nation.

His defeat becomes almost unthinkable because it would mean not merely that one politician lost, but that the people themselves were rejected.

This is how a republic begins dreaming of a king without ever using the word.

The Constitution remains. The flag remains. The election remains.

But the relationship between the leader and the law begins to invert.

The law is no longer the standard by which the leader is judged.

The leader becomes the emotional standard by which the law is judged.

The king is not merely the man above the law.

He is the man whose every encounter with the law is interpreted as an offense against him.

And there, beneath all the talk of kings and republics, is the same one-page order.

The government seal.

The signature.

The former lawyer.

The shield.

The conflict is not hidden in philosophy.

It is written into the paperwork.

A country born from the conviction that rulers must answer to something outside themselves watched its president pursue special protection for himself, his family, and his business empire.

The law says the ruler must be auditable.

The ruler says the audit is persecution.

The republic says no man is king.

The man says every institution that limits him is corrupt.

A country founded in rebellion against kings becomes fascinated by the man who behaves most like one.

Not because Trump is foreign to America.

Because he is recognizably American.

He is wealth without embarrassment, victory without restraint, celebrity without distance, grievance without limit.

He is the fantasy of total freedom from people who might say no.

The self-made man enlarged until the self becomes a government.

That is why this story cannot end with Trump.

He did not invent the desire to escape judgment.

He gave it a face.

He turned a private human impulse into a national spectacle.

Every one of us understands, at some level, the wish to become our own final authority.

That does not make the private evasions of an ordinary person morally equivalent to presidential self-dealing. Power changes the scale, the danger, and the consequences.

But the mechanisms rhyme.

Every one of us has something we would rather not submit to the audit.

A belief. A leader. A relationship. A country. A wound. A story about who we are and why we became this way.

We protect the things that protect us.

A political identity can protect us from uncertainty. A religion can protect us from meaninglessness. A grievance can protect us from shame. A relationship can protect us from loneliness. A story about ourselves can protect us from admitting that we caused some of our own suffering.

Once a story becomes necessary to our survival, evidence against it begins to feel less like information than danger.

We do not examine the evidence.

We defend the shelter.

Someone betrays us, and the betrayal becomes proof that no one can be trusted. Someone lies about us, and every future criticism becomes another lie. An institution fails us, and the failure becomes proof that all institutions are corrupt.

Something real happens.

Then we build a world around it.

A wound deserves recognition. It may deserve justice. It may require protection, repair, or restitution.

But it cannot be allowed to determine everything that becomes true afterward.

Your wound deserves recognition. It does not deserve sovereignty.

Being wronged does not make every suspicion accurate, every enemy evil, or every retaliation moral.

Being wronged does not make everything you do afterward right.

Suffering is evidence. It proves that something happened.

It does not prove everything we come to believe because it happened.

A wound can explain a worldview without validating it. A grievance can reveal injustice without becoming a license for domination. A history of persecution can demand protection without granting permanent innocence.

The audit asks us to preserve that distinction.

What happened?

What did it mean?

What does it actually justify?

Where does the truth end and the story begin?

Those questions hurt because the story is often protecting more than the wound.

It is protecting the self.

To revise the story may mean admitting that someone we hated was partly right. That someone we loved was partly wrong. That a cause we served became corrupted. That a country we believed in betrayed its principles. That a leader who understood our pain also exploited it.

It may mean admitting that the person who hurt us did not cause everything we became afterward.

We were not only acted upon.

We also acted.

This is what makes the audit feel like an attack.

It separates explanation from absolution.

It says:

Yes, this happened to you.

And you are still responsible for what you made from it.

That is not cruelty.

It is adulthood.

It is also how a society learns.

A healthy person is not someone who is never wrong. A healthy movement is not one whose leaders never fail. A healthy country is not one with an innocent history.

The decisive quality is not perfection.

It is correctability.

Can the person receive information that threatens the story? Can the movement condemn its own leader? Can the institution expose its own abuse? Can the country examine its own mythology?

Can the believer let reality revise belief?

Without that capacity, intelligence becomes rationalization. Loyalty becomes concealment. Faith becomes denial. Patriotism becomes mythology.

A system that cannot be corrected drifts farther from reality. The error compounds. The explanations grow more elaborate. The enemies multiply. Maintaining the story requires more punishment, more loyalty, and more control.

Eventually the system must either change or begin attacking whatever reveals the need for change.

This is why freedom requires the willingness to be corrected.

We often imagine freedom as the absence of restraint: no one telling us what to do, no authority above us, no obstacle between desire and action.

But total freedom from correction is not freedom.

It is isolation from reality.

A person who cannot be corrected becomes trapped inside the self. A movement that cannot be corrected becomes trapped inside ideology. A country that cannot be corrected becomes trapped inside myth. A ruler who cannot be corrected becomes trapped inside power.

And everyone beneath him becomes trapped there too.

The audit is not the enemy of freedom.

It is one of freedom’s conditions.

It creates a space where a lie can be identified before it becomes a system, where a grievance can be honored before it becomes a weapon, where a citizen can say:

I believed this.

I defended this.

I may still believe part of it.

But this did not pass.

That is not weakness.

It is the courage to remain in contact with reality.

A free society depends on leaders who remain auditable.

But it also depends on citizens who remain auditable.

Institutions can investigate a president. Courts can issue rulings. Journalists can expose facts. Elections can remove leaders.

But none of those mechanisms can preserve a republic if millions of people have already decided that every unfavorable result is illegitimate.

The institution can conduct the audit.

The citizen still decides whether the audit is allowed to mean anything.

That is why the deepest threat is not merely a leader who refuses to submit to judgment.

It is the moment his followers begin believing that asking him to submit to reality is itself an injustice.

At that point, accountability becomes persecution. Correction becomes betrayal. The law becomes an enemy.

And the leader moves beyond the reach of every standard except loyalty.

This is what makes the IRS story larger than taxes.

A president was genuinely wronged by someone inside the government.

That injury deserved justice.

Instead, the injury became part of an attempt to secure protections for the president, his family, and his business world that ordinary people could never arrange for themselves.

The audit became the offense.

The auditor became the enemy.

The man seeking exemption became the victim of anyone who objected.

And his supporters were invited to interpret the shield not as corruption, but protection.

That is how the unauditable leader is made.

Not by one document, one lawyer, or one corrupt act.

By a story strong enough to make every external judgment appear corrupt before it is heard.

By a movement willing to protect the leader from the meaning of the evidence.

By citizens who no longer ask whether the man passed the audit.

Only whether the audit was loyal to the man.

Trump is the spectacle.

The temptation is human.

Everyone has something they do not want audited: the leader they love, the wound that explains them, the country that gives them identity, the cause that gives them purpose, the story that allows them to remain innocent.

The question is not whether we possess such things.

The question is whether anything outside us is still allowed to examine them.

Can the evidence contradict us?

Can the law restrain us?

Can an enemy tell the truth?

Can a friend expose a lie?

Can our suffering be real without making every conclusion born from it true?

Can the person we believe in fail?

Can we?

A republic survives only while the answer remains yes.

Because no leader gets to be his own auditor.

No movement gets to certify its own righteousness.
