How Trump Works

A guide to understanding the person, the appeal, and the power.

Trump is a salesman who sells Trump.

His name carries a promise of wealth, strength, and success. In politics, the offer becomes personal: he will fight for you, defeat the people holding you back, and make his victories yours.

Looking powerful can persuade people to cooperate with him. Their cooperation can give him real power. But getting people to follow him does not guarantee that he will deliver what he promised them. Understanding Trump means keeping those differences in view.

Winning what?

Trump pursues several things that can look like winning: money, office, policy changes, admiration, and getting someone to do what he wants. Sometimes these aims support one another. Sometimes he gets one and loses another.

After losing the 2020 election, he tried to remain president while insisting he had won. Many supporters accepted his story, but he still left office in January 2021. Preserving their belief in his victory did not preserve his presidency.

The distinction matters in smaller encounters too. If he demands that someone change a decision, an insult that dominates the news does not tell us whether the decision changed. The attention is real. So is the difference between obtaining a concession and announcing victory.

Public recognition matters to him in its own right. He repeatedly promotes his accomplishments and answers criticism by attacking the critic. A question about whether he made a mistake can become a fight over who looks stronger, smarter, or more deserving of respect. That fight may draw attention away from the original question.

Counterattack is also a practiced way of dealing with resistance. His former lawyer Roy Cohn modeled aggressive attacks and refusal to concede; Trump has repeatedly used confrontation to attract attention and try to make people give way. A response can be both personally satisfying and useful without every word having been planned.

A public challenge therefore often brings a counterattack. Whether the attack helps him obtain what he wanted is a separate question.

When facts become tests of loyalty

An unwelcome fact can threaten both something Trump wants and the picture of himself he is presenting. He often disputes the fact, plays it down, or attacks the person reporting it. The argument can then shift from whether the information is true to whether the person supplying it is on his side.

In February 2020, he privately told journalist Bob Woodward that the coronavirus spread through the air and was dangerous. Publicly, he predicted that known U.S. cases would soon approach zero, while also acknowledging that the situation could worsen. He later told Woodward he wanted to play down the danger to avoid panic. He knew more about the danger than his reassurance suggested. Avoiding panic was his stated reason; the reassurance also presented him as a leader with the situation under control.

After the 2020 election, officials repeatedly explained why the evidence did not support his claim that fraud had changed the result. He continued promoting the claim. Calling the election stolen made his effort to remain president sound like correcting an injustice: others were being asked to rescue a victory rather than overturn a defeat. An official who rejected that account could be treated as disloyal instead of as someone reporting an unwelcome finding.

Mike Pence’s experience shows the consequences. As vice president, Pence presided over Congress’s formal count of electoral votes. That role did not authorize him to reject state-certified votes and choose the winner. Trump nevertheless wanted him to reject votes for Biden or delay the count. Pence refused, and Trump treated the refusal as disloyalty. A legal duty became a personal betrayal in Trump’s account.

This is especially consequential when Trump expects someone’s job or organization to serve his interests even though its duties require independence. The boundary exists outside government too. In 2019, he admitted misusing his charitable foundation’s funds for political purposes and paid $2 million in court-ordered damages. A charity bearing his name was still a charity.

When he attacks someone who supplies an unwelcome fact, the fact still needs an answer. You do not have to decide whether he believes his own bullshit before checking the claim—or noticing what it asks you to excuse or support.

Relationships can change when the offer changes

A former enemy can become useful; a longtime ally can become an obstacle. Present cooperation can matter more than the history of a relationship.

J. D. Vance went from fierce critic to supporter and eventually Trump’s choice for running mate. At a 2022 rally, Trump promoted Vance’s Senate candidacy while declaring that Vance was “kissing my ass.” The help and the public assertion of superiority arrived together: Trump could advance Vance’s career while emphasizing Vance’s need for him.

If you expect loyalty to accumulate like a pension, this is confusing. Past criticism need not prevent a useful alliance, and years of service may not protect someone who refuses an important demand.

Trump can also present past help as a debt. In 2023, he described his endorsement as decisive in Ron DeSantis’s rise to Florida governor, then called DeSantis disloyal for challenging him for the presidency. Whether Trump deserved all that credit is a separate question. His claim made DeSantis’s challenge sound like ingratitude rather than an ordinary political contest.

Cooperation does not always require public submission. In November 2025, Trump held a cordial meeting with New York mayor-elect Zohran Mamdani, a democratic socialist who had sharply criticized him. They discussed housing costs and public safety while retaining sharp political differences. Their disagreement did not prevent a friendly discussion of shared practical concerns. That did not establish a lasting agreement, but it showed that past hostility need not determine the next response.

He can also acknowledge a personal mistake. Discussing the pressure he had put on his older brother to enter the family business rather than become a pilot, Trump said in 2019, “I do regret having put pressure on him.” His more familiar refusal to concede should not erase that acknowledgment.

What do different supporters get from him?

Trump’s ability involves more than attracting attention. He gives people a vivid account of what they lack, who is responsible, and how joining him could change their position. The offer can be protection, restored respect, a stronger country, a desired policy, or a share in the success he displays.

Some of that offer rests on long-standing political beliefs. For decades, he has described America as exploited by other countries and badly represented by weak leaders. Better trade terms and greater defense spending by allies fit that picture: the country has been pushed around, and he will get it a better deal. These views predate his presidential campaigns. They cannot be explained simply as whatever attracted applause that day.

He connects aspirations with fears. His 2015 campaign launch described immigrants arriving from Mexico through crime, drugs, and rape, while conceding some were good people. Presenting a group as dangerous made action against it sound protective. The same speech promised to restore the American dream. He was offering both an explanation of what threatened people’s lives and a promise that their lives could improve.

Recognizing what will resonate is an ability. It does not establish that he has correctly identified the cause of a problem or knows how to solve it. Someone can feel understood by a promise that will not help them. Someone else can receive a benefit from a politician whose manner they dislike.

For some supporters, his defiance feels like their own strength. Where another listener hears cruelty in an insult, they hear a powerful person confronting an institution they distrust. If they believe influential people have treated them unfairly, his refusal to follow those people’s conventions can itself look like evidence that he represents them. The conduct has not changed. Its meaning to the listener has.

He makes this identification explicit. While denouncing criminal charges against him in 2023, he said: “They’re not coming after me, they’re coming after you.” Defending him can then feel like defending one’s community. A correction about him may be heard as an attack on the people he claims to protect.

Other supporters want policies, enjoy the show, trust his judgment, or dislike the alternative more. In a July 2024 Pew survey, 30 percent of his supporters liked his conduct, while 88 percent agreed with him on many or all issues. Admiration and toleration can produce the same vote.

When defeating the other party feels essential, voters may excuse conduct they would punish in an opponent. Research finds this willingness among supporters of both parties. But defending a false claim does not necessarily mean knowingly excusing a lie. Research on the 2020 election found substantial sincere belief or suspicion that it had been stolen. Someone who believes that claim may think Trump is defending democracy by challenging the result. Sincerity does not make the claim true, but it changes what the person thinks they are supporting.

These different reasons for participating give Trump power. People vote, donate, campaign, defend his account, and make opposition costly for other politicians. Their support supplies resources and authority; what they expect in return can also limit what they will accept. Feeling defended and being materially helped remain separate questions, and different supporters will judge delivery by different standards.

Selling success and delivering it

Trump’s reputation can make his abilities appear broader than his record warrants. The Apprentice presented him as a decisive business authority. Producer Bill Pruitt later described editing that helped create the impression of a boss who always knew what he was doing. His actual record includes completed projects and successful promotion alongside inherited advantages, financial rescues, and bankrupt businesses.

His Taj Mahal casino opened in 1990 with borrowing costs that a former regulator said the business could not support. People were delighted to see Donald. Their delight did not pay the interest.

Selling the winner is a business skill. It is not every business skill.

Government makes that distinction more consequential. A president’s confidence may persuade people to back a project. Delivering the promised result then depends on how well the work is planned, funded, and carried out. He can back capable people whose work produces a genuine achievement. Operation Warp Speed helped accelerate COVID-19 vaccine development through government financing and cooperation among agencies, scientists, and companies. That achievement belongs alongside his misleading reassurance about the virus.

Other participants also bring purposes of their own. Trump’s move from supporting abortion rights to opposing them connected him with groups seeking conservative judges. He nominated justices, senators confirmed them, and his three appointees joined the 2022 majority overturning Roe v. Wade, removing federal constitutional protection for abortion. His changed position helped produce a lasting legal consequence because an organized movement and other officials helped carry it through.

Cooperation can cross party lines too. In 2018, he signed the bipartisan First Step Act, reducing some federal sentencing penalties and expanding programs preparing prisoners for release.

These achievements matter when assessing what he can accomplish. He does not have to master every task personally to support useful work, and other people can use his prominence to obtain results they value. The practical question is whether a promise has the people, preparation, and cooperation needed to become a result.

What makes pressure work—or fail

As president, Trump can threaten interests that would be beyond his reach as a celebrity or businessman. Whether people give way depends partly on what refusal could cost them and what help they have in resisting.

In March 2025, an order targeting the law firm Paul Weiss threatened restrictions affecting its government access and clients. The firm agreed to changes and pledged $40 million in free legal work; Trump withdrew the order. Its chairman said clients might leave rather than risk trouble with the administration, even if the firm initially won protection in court. The threat could damage the business before a final legal decision.

The agreement showed the power of that pressure. It did not establish that the accusations against the firm were justified, and the pledge was not a cash payment to Trump. Obtaining cooperation and proving a claim are different accomplishments.

People who can withhold something he needs may be better placed to resist. In January 2021, Trump considered installing a Justice Department leader willing to lend the department’s authority to his election-fraud claims. Senior officials threatened to resign together. According to their testimony, Trump concluded that the proposed leader would be unable to accomplish the plan without them and backed away from the appointment.

Their refusal created an obstacle that replacing one person would not solve. But Trump abandoned that appointment, not his effort to resist the election result. A limit can stop a particular method without changing the larger aim.

Who holds these positions therefore matters. A demand that fails against officials prepared to refuse may succeed with replacements willing to carry it out. Conversely, support that makes refusal less costly can make a target harder to pressure. Trump’s wishes alone do not determine the outcome: other people turn demands into action, negotiate their terms, or prevent them from being carried out.

Four useful questions

When a new episode arrives, these questions can help keep the event understandable:

• What does he want here? Look for the particular decision, benefit, recognition, or concession he is seeking. If several aims are visible, keep them separate.
• What version of events is he asking people to accept? Notice how that account makes his demand seem deserved—and whether the evidence supports it.
• Whose cooperation does he need? Consider what those people want, what they can withhold, and what agreeing or refusing would cost them.
• What actually happened? Check the decision, action, or result rather than treating either his boast or an opponent’s outrage as the outcome.

Read together, the answers connect what he wants and says with what other people enable him to do. They will not explain every decision or reveal every private motive. They can keep the promise, the performance, and the result from merging into one very entertaining blur.
