The Life Inside Life

What if we’ve misunderstood what spirit means?

A note before the piece

This piece began with a question that had been sitting with me: What is it going to take for America to understand spirit?

As I thought about it, I realized that part of the problem may be the word itself. Many people hear spirit or spirituality and think of religion, superstition, or claims that conflict with reason. But what if spirit is simply one name for something we all experience, even if we describe it differently?

I wanted to explore whether the divide between secular and spiritual people may sometimes be a failure of language—and whether a clearer understanding of spirit could help us recognize the depth, value, and aliveness within ourselves, one another, and the world.

This piece is also a natural continuation of Deep and Wide: an attempt to understand what inward development might actually lead us toward.

⸻

What is it going to take for America to understand spirit?

Maybe first, a better definition.

For many people, the word itself is the problem. They hear spirit and think of ghosts, superstition, organized religion, or someone asking them to believe something that cannot be proved. They reject that idea and assume they have rejected spirituality too.

But spirit may not be as strange as it sounds.

By spirit, I mean the inward aliveness of reality: the fact that life is not only something we can observe from the outside, but something being lived from within.

A body can be measured. A person can be described by their biology, opinions, résumé, economic value, or practical use. But none of those descriptions contains the experience of being that person—their love, fear, memory, imagination, pain, or sense of presence.

That inner dimension is what I mean by spirit.

Others may call parts of it consciousness, presence, God, nature, life force, love, or the sacred. These words are not identical. But sometimes they are different attempts to describe something people have encountered in common: awareness, connection, awe, or the sense that reality is deeper than its visible surface.

We may be arguing over language while standing inside the same mystery.

One person stands in nature and feels the boundary of the self loosen. They call it God. Another calls it awe. Another calls it a response in the brain and nervous system.

The explanations differ.

The experience still happened.

We do not have to agree on what it ultimately proves before admitting that it is real.

This is where secular and spiritual people often lose one another.

Spiritual people sometimes make claims they cannot support and expect everyone else to accept them. Secular people sometimes treat anything that cannot be measured from the outside as unreal or unimportant.

Both can miss something.

Science gives us extraordinary knowledge about the mechanisms of life. It can show us what happens in the brain when we listen to music, grieve, fall in love, pray, or meditate.

But the measurement is not the experience.

A brain scan is not the feeling of being broken open by a song. A chemical description of love is not the same thing as loving someone. An explanation of grief does not contain the person who was lost.

The outside is real.

So is the inside.

Maybe spirituality begins with taking the inside seriously.

It does not have to mean joining a religion, abandoning reason, or pretending certainty about things no one fully understands. It can begin with a simpler recognition: life has an interior. Consciousness matters. A person is more than an object moving through the world.

This may sound obvious.

Much of American life is organized as though it were not true.

We turn people into workers, consumers, voters, audiences, enemies, and brands. We turn nature into property and raw material. We turn attention into a product and identity into a performance.

We ask what something is worth, what it can produce, how it can be used, and whether it can be scaled.

Those questions have their place.

But they are not enough.

When a culture loses contact with spirit, everything begins to look usable.

That may be part of America’s deeper crisis. We have developed immense power over the external world without developing an equal understanding of the life using that power.

We know how to build, extract, accelerate, automate, broadcast, and consume.

We are less certain how to be present. How to recognize value that cannot be priced. How to live with mystery. How to decide what all this power is for.

We developed wide without developing deep.

Spirit is one name for what we encounter when we begin moving inward.

Beneath the identity we perform, there is awareness. Beneath consumption, there is longing. Beneath the story that we are separate and self-made, there is relationship.

None of us created ourselves.

We are shaped by other people, by history, by nature, by language, by love, by wounds, and by forces we barely understand.

We are individuals.

We are not isolated.

Without language for spirit, we struggle to describe the difference between a person and a thing, between being alive and merely functioning, between using the world and belonging to it.

We lose the ability to name what makes something worthy of care even when it is not useful to us.

The sacred does not have to mean magical. It can mean that which should never be treated as merely an instrument.

A person is sacred because they are more than what they can do for us. A forest is sacred because its value exceeds what can be extracted from it. Attention is sacred because our lives become what we repeatedly give it to.

America does not need everyone to accept one religion, one explanation of reality, or even one word.

But we may need to understand that life has an interior—and that different people sometimes use different language to describe their encounters with it.

Spirit may not be something America needs to discover.

It may be what becomes visible when we stop reducing life to what it can produce, explain, or provide, and begin meeting it as life.

Spirit has not disappeared.

We have forgotten how to recognize the life inside life.
