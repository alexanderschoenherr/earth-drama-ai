Human. Artificial. Human.

What artificial intelligence may demand from the intelligence that created it

Human beings have begun doing something very strange.

For almost all of history, intelligence was something we encountered in living things.

An animal watching the grass move.

A child learning a language.

A mathematician staring at a problem.

We built tools that extended nearly everything else about us. Tools made us stronger. Telescopes let us see farther. Cars moved us faster. Computers expanded memory and calculation.

And eventually human intelligence became sophisticated enough to begin building intelligence itself.

Artificial intelligence.

That sentence has become so normal that it is easy to miss how weird it is.

Natural intelligence appeared on Earth.

Natural intelligence learned enough about the world—and eventually enough about intelligence—to begin constructing another form of intelligence outside itself.

And now we are trying to make it more powerful.

Which is where things get complicated.

Because intelligence is not just another useful capability.

It sits behind almost everything else we do.

Science. Engineering. Strategy. Medicine. Software. Art. War.

A machine that becomes better at forms of intelligence can become useful across many forms of human power.

So countries want it.

Companies want it.

Governments are afraid of falling behind.

And almost immediately, one of the oldest human problems appears inside one of our newest technologies.

Imagine the United States decides that some new level of AI capability is dangerous enough that we should slow down until we understand it better.

That might be wise.

But then someone asks:

What if China doesn’t?

Now the question is no longer simply:

Should we be careful?

It is:

Can we afford to be careful if they aren’t?

China can ask the same question about us.

AI companies can ask it about one another.

And suddenly we arrive at a strange paradox.

The more powerful AI appears capable of becoming, the stronger the argument for caution becomes.

But the more powerful it appears capable of becoming, the more frightening it becomes to let someone else get there first.

The same fact produces two opposite instructions.

Slow down.

Hurry up.

This is how very intelligent people can become trapped inside a system producing an unintelligent result.

America accelerates because it fears China.

China sees America accelerating and has more reason to fear America.

One company speeds up because another company is speeding up.

Everybody experiences themselves as reacting.

But everybody is also becoming part of what everybody else is reacting to.

I become stronger because I am afraid of you.

You cannot see my fear.

You can only see that I am becoming stronger.

So you become stronger too.

Then I look at you and think:

See?

I was right.

There is an obvious sentence waiting for anyone who suggests restraint:

If we don’t do it, someone else will.

The irritating thing about this sentence is that sometimes it is true.

One country cannot safely assume that another country will accept whatever limits it accepts for itself.

But there is another possibility.

If everyone believes someone else will do something, everyone can do it and then point to the result as proof that nobody had a choice.

Sometimes inevitability is real.

Sometimes we manufacture it together.

And that is where this stops being merely a problem of artificial intelligence.

It becomes a problem of human intelligence.

Because what kind of intelligence does it take to solve a problem like this?

We clearly possess extraordinary technical intelligence.

We can split atoms.

Map genomes.

Land machines on other planets.

Build computers that generate language.

We are extremely good at answering:

Can we do it?

But greater capability creates another kind of question.

Can rivals recognize that their attempts to make themselves safer may be making the other side more afraid?

Can they cooperate without pretending they completely trust each other?

Can we notice when something that makes sense for every individual player is producing an outcome that makes no sense for everyone?

Can we build systems in which responsible behavior does not require one side to make itself uniquely vulnerable?

Raw cognitive power does not automatically answer these questions.

They require judgment.

Foresight.

Self-knowledge.

Coordination.

The ability to understand the system you are inside while you are still inside it.

Perhaps wisdom is not the same thing as intelligence.

But intelligence without wisdom becomes increasingly dangerous as its power grows.

And now the story begins to turn back toward us.

At first, it looks like human beings are developing artificial intelligence.

We are.

But developing artificial intelligence creates new kinds of power.

And those powers create problems that demand further development from the human beings holding them.

Not because AI sits us down and teaches us wisdom.

Not because there is a spiritual master hiding somewhere inside the server rack.

We should probably maintain some standards.

It happens because greater power changes what is required of the being that possesses it.

⸻

Human intelligence creates artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence creates new power.

That power reveals limitations in the humans who created it.

And those limitations create a new demand:

develop.

Human.

Artificial.

Human again.

Pull the camera back far enough and the symmetry becomes difficult to ignore.

Life appeared on Earth.

Over an almost incomprehensible span of time, life became capable of sensing the world.

Then remembering it.

Learning from it.

Imagining things that did not yet exist.

Eventually one species became capable of understanding nature well enough to begin rearranging it intentionally.

We built tools.

Then machines.

Then computers.

And eventually natural intelligence began constructing artificial intelligence.

Now the artificial intelligence created by natural intelligence is changing the conditions under which natural intelligence itself must develop.

We made something outside ourselves.

And in making it, we may have created a mirror.

It reflects our brilliance.

Our fear.

Our hunger for power.

Our strange ability to understand astonishingly complicated things while remaining capable of creating very simple problems for one another.

The deeper challenge presented by AI may therefore not be simply how intelligent the machine becomes.

It may be what the existence of that intelligence reveals about ours.

Can our judgment develop alongside our capability?

Can we build forms of cooperation sophisticated enough to survive competition?

Can we become powerful without requiring everyone else to become powerless?

⸻

None of this is guaranteed.

Artificial intelligence could become vastly more capable while human beings become less capable of governing the systems they create.

Artificial intelligence could develop.

Human intelligence could fail to keep pace.

That may be the real danger.

Not simply that our machines become too intelligent.

That our power becomes greater than our capacity to live intelligently with it.

We have spent thousands of years learning how to become more capable.

Artificial intelligence may be presenting us with a harder problem:

how to become capable of being this capable.

Human intelligence has become capable of creating intelligence outside itself.

And the existence of that artificial intelligence has now created a new developmental demand on the intelligence that made it.

There is no guarantee we meet that demand.

But if we do, something remarkable will have happened.

Our development of artificial intelligence will have become part of the development of human intelligence.

Human.

Artificial.

Human again.
