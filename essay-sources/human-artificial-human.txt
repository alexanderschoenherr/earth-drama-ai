Human. Artificial. Human.
What if the intelligence we create changes the intelligence that created it?

Human intelligence is building artificial intelligence.

I keep wondering whether we’re stopping the sentence too soon.

Usually we follow that story into the machine. What can it learn? What will it be able to do? Will it become smarter than the people who made it?

Fair questions.

But look at the people for a moment.

We have reached a point where someone can be irritated that the intelligence we built is taking too long to write an email. There is something almost reassuring about our ability to make an astonishing development immediately annoying.

Meanwhile, we are making these systems more capable.

AI can help people write software, analyze research, design technologies, make decisions.

Intelligence is strange that way. It doesn’t just give us another capability. It can help us create capabilities.

So we are not only increasing what AI can do.

We are increasing what humans can do with it.

Which brings us to a problem we understand considerably better than we understand intelligence:

Someone else might have more of it.

Suppose the United States develops a new AI system that could provide a major advantage, but there are serious reasons to think deploying it too quickly could be dangerous.

Maybe the sensible thing is to test it longer.

But what if China has something similar and keeps going?

Now caution has a cost.

China can ask the same question about us.

The same power supplies both instructions.

Slow down.

Hurry up.

I become stronger because I am afraid of you.

You may even understand that. You still have to deal with the fact that I am getting stronger.

So you become stronger too.

Then I look at you and think:

See? I was right.

All that intelligence, and we can still end up here.

The problem exists between us.

Maybe what is missing is something neither of us can do alone.

Suppose we agree on a limit.

We do not trust each other enough for “Okay, promise?”

Fair.

So we build a way to check.

The first version fails. Someone finds a loophole. We change it. Eventually we have something we did not have before: a way for rivals to know whether the other is keeping its word without simply having to trust them.

Nobody’s IQ went up.

Nobody became a saint.

The fear may still be there.

But together, we learned how to do something we could not reliably do before.

And if that method survives—if it becomes a practice or institution—we do not have to invent it from zero the next time.

The solution has become part of what humans know how to do.

I think that counts as human intelligence too.

So maybe the sentence keeps going.

Human intelligence creates artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence changes what humans can do.

That changes the problems humans have to solve.

And solving those problems can change what humans are capable of.

AI does not have to want any of this.

It doesn’t even have to teach us.

Humans have been doing versions of this forever. We build parts of the world, then have to learn how to live inside what we built.

But AI makes the loop particularly strange.

Because one of the things we are now building into our environment is intelligence itself.

Intelligence arose in living creatures.

Eventually, one of them built machines that could perform parts of the work intelligence does.

Now those machines are becoming part of the environment in which their creators have to think, choose, compete, cooperate, and decide what comes next.

There is something about that return I can’t quite get past.

And there is no promise it makes us better.

Maybe competition teaches us to rush.

Maybe distrust teaches us secrecy.

Maybe increasingly capable systems let us pursue the same frightened objectives more efficiently.

Artificial intelligence could develop while our ability to live intelligently with it goes backward.

Failure doesn’t sit outside the loop.

It shapes what gets built next too.

We created something that changes the problems human intelligence has to solve.

How we solve them—or fail to—can change what humans become capable of.

And then we build again.

The intelligence we created may become part of what changes the intelligence that created it.

Human.

Artificial.

Human again.
