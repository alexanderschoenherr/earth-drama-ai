We Are Still Among the Causes

What AI doomerism gets wrong about an unfinished future

“Isn’t it already too late?”

Maybe.

I think that has to be allowed.

Maybe AI really does become something we cannot control. Maybe the companies keep racing, governments fail to coordinate, the safety work arrives late, and humanity discovers that creating something smarter than itself was one of those ideas that looked better in the meeting.

I don’t know.

Neither do you.

And that is where I start having trouble with we’re doomed.

“This could kill us” is a warning.

“This probably will kill us” is a prediction.

“There is nothing meaningful left for anyone to do about it” is another claim entirely.

We keep saying them as though they are one sentence.

They aren’t.

You could think there is a 90 percent chance this goes terribly and still spend your life trying to move it to 89.

If the stakes are humanity, one percent is a very large one percent.

Fine.

Then the probability cuts both ways.

You cannot say:

Even a small chance of extinction is too important to ignore.

and then:

There’s only a small chance anything works, so why bother?

Apparently small probabilities matter around here.

Good.

Let’s keep them.

The phrase I keep hearing is too late.

Too late for what?

Too late to stop AI research entirely?

Maybe.

Too late to prevent increasingly powerful systems from being built?

Maybe.

Too late to guarantee nothing dangerous ever gets deployed?

Different question.

Too late to reduce the risk?

Different.

Too late to change an incentive, delay a deployment, discover something we do not currently know, create an agreement that does not currently exist?

Different.

Too late for literally anything human beings do from here to alter what happens?

That is a hell of a claim.

Sometimes it really is too late.

Doors close. Some mistakes cannot be unmade. There are things you can prevent on Tuesday that you can only respond to on Friday.

Nothing I’m saying requires pretending otherwise.

But late is not a synonym for finished.

The situation gets worse.

Possibilities disappear.

We miss opportunities we should have taken.

And somewhere along the way:

We are losing

becomes:

We have already lost.

Those are not the same thing.

And once you believe the second one, your behavior changes.

Why keep searching for a solution you know cannot exist?

Why cooperate if you know cooperation will fail?

Why accept a disadvantage for safety if somebody else will build the thing anyway?

Why regulate an inevitable technology?

You can sit down.

Or, strangely, you can sprint.

Because AI is inevitable can produce two opposite reactions:

We can’t stop it, so give up.

and:

We can’t stop it, so we’d better get there first.

One produces resignation.

One produces acceleration.

Same premise:

The future has already escaped human choice.

A company says it has to move fast because its competitors will.

Its competitors say the same thing.

A country says it cannot slow down because another country won’t.

The other country says the same thing.

Everybody points at everybody else as the reason nobody has a choice.

Eventually the pattern gets a name.

The race.

And now the race starts telling everybody what to do.

This is one of humanity’s better tricks.

We do things.

Enough of us do the things.

The things become a system.

Then the system comes back and says:

Sorry, guys. Nothing I can do.

To be fair, systems really can become larger than any individual inside them.

Incentives are real. Competition is real. One engineer cannot personally cancel geopolitics before lunch.

But there is a difference between:

I do not control this

and

this cannot be influenced.

Most human agency has never looked like total control.

It looks like changing an incentive. Creating a rule. Building an agreement. Refusing something. Buying time. Moving a probability.

Finding where reality still bends.

And sometimes the “we” capable of doing that does not exist yet.

It has to be built.

But what is true now does not automatically become what must be true forever.

AI capabilities keep advancing.

The incentives stay terrible forever.

Coordination stays impossible forever.

Human beings remain exactly as capable of responding to the problem as they were before the problem fully arrived.

Why does only the machine get a future?

And this is where the belief itself starts to matter.

If everybody believes the race cannot be slowed, slowing down becomes foolish.

If slowing down becomes foolish, fewer people do it.

If fewer people do it, the race becomes harder to slow.

Then everybody looks at the race and says:

See? Unstoppable.

Sometimes the thing really was unstoppable.

Sometimes the belief got there first.

Something can still be changeable now and genuinely unchangeable later.

Treat it as unchangeable during the window when it still responds, and eventually you may be right.

Premature fatalism can manufacture lateness.

Not magically.

If I believe hard enough that my toaster is a Bengal tiger, breakfast remains disappointing.

Reality gets a vote.

But beliefs change behavior.

Behavior changes institutions.

Institutions change incentives.

Incentives change decisions.

Decisions change probabilities.

When the future depends partly on human behavior, beliefs about the future are not standing outside the system.

They are among its inputs.

And that is where I think hope gets misunderstood.

Hope is usually presented as the soft position.

Everything is going to be okay.

I don’t mean that.

Everything may not be okay.

Hope does not know how this ends. That’s the point.

Fatalism says:

I know.

Hope says:

I don’t.

Which sounds weaker until you remember that none of us has actually been to the future.

Hope does not require you to look at terrible odds and secretly turn them into better odds because you need to sleep tonight.

You can think:

I don’t think this works. I think we should try anyway.

There may be too little probability for comfort and more than enough probability for responsibility.

And maybe that is why hopelessness can be strangely comforting.

If nothing can be done, nothing is required of you.

If the ending is fixed, you cannot be the person who chose wrong. You cannot be the person who failed to act when action mattered.

Fatalism is horrifying.

It is also, in one very specific way, restful.

Hope is more annoying.

Hope says:

Maybe this still depends partly on us.

Oh.

Now we may have to do something.

Cooperate with people we dislike.

Give something up.

Try something with bad odds.

Accept some restraint.

Change our minds.

Spend years working on a problem without knowing whether the work succeeds.

Hope does not let us off the hook.

There is a difference between:

I feel hopeless

and:

There is no hope.

The first tells me something about a person.

The second tells me something about reality.

Reality owes us evidence.

Because we’re doomed does not only describe a future.

It can give us permission in the present.

Permission to stop.

Permission to race.

Permission to say somebody else would do it anyway.

Permission to inhabit catastrophe before catastrophe arrives.

And human beings are perfectly capable of doing that while also doing completely ordinary things.

Possible human extinction.

401(k).

Superintelligence.

Buy detergent.

Dentist at 2:30.

Someone can sincerely believe civilization may not have much of a future and still get furious because DoorDash forgot the fries.

But there is a real danger in letting an imagined ending drain meaning backward out of the life we actually have.

Maybe the future disappears someday.

It hasn’t today.

And if catastrophe does come, it gets to take that future then.

It does not also need the years before it.

A catastrophe can take the future once. Fatalism can let it take the present in advance.

But we do not know that we are at an ending.

That is the point.

Maybe the danger is enormous.

Tell me.

Maybe the odds are terrible.

Tell me.

Maybe we have already lost opportunities we desperately wish we still had.

Tell me exactly which ones.

Then tell me what is still responsive.

What can still move?

What can still be learned?

What can still be refused?

What can still be changed?

What probability can still be altered?

If nothing can still change, then that is something we have to face.

But that conclusion needs more than fear.

More than momentum.

More than the fact that everybody seems to be running.

More than the fact that we cannot currently see the solution.

I cannot see a path is not the same sentence as there is no path.

Maybe someday they become the same sentence.

Until then, hope does not need to promise us anything.

It is not another prophecy.

It is our refusal to finish the future early.

Maybe we lose.

Maybe we don’t.

But as long as what we do can still affect what happens, we are not only the audience.

We are still among the causes.
